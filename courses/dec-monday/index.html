Manager Monday · The manager lens · The Vanderbilt Manager Standard

The standard you can witness.

Vanderbilt has written down what a good manager does here, and every line is something a colleague could watch you do. This session walks the list, teaches the witness test that separates behavior from intention, and asks you to place yourself on it honestly.

By the end of this session, you will be able to

  1. Name the observable behaviors that define the Vanderbilt manager standard
  2. Tell a demonstrable behavior apart from an intention or a personality trait
  3. Locate yourself against the standard with evidence and pick one behavior to grow

Section 01 · The List

Written so a colleague could watch it happen.

Plenty of leadership models describe who a manager should be. The Vanderbilt standard does something more useful: it lists what a manager here does, in lines a colleague could watch happen. That design choice is the whole point of this section.

The witness test

Every line of the Vanderbilt manager standard passes one test: a colleague could watch you do it. Holding the 1:1, giving written feedback, naming the expectation out loud. If nobody could witness it, it is not on the list.

In the room

Most leadership language describes who a manager should be: caring, strategic, approachable. The Vanderbilt standard refuses that language. Every line names a doing: hold the 1:1, deliver the written feedback, say the expectation out loud. If nobody could watch it happen, it did not make the list.

Talk it through: Which line of the standard surprised you by being there, and which quality you value most is missing because nobody could witness it?

Quick check: which of these is written the way the standard is written?

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: pick one line of the standard. When did a colleague last see you do it? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Pick one line of the standard and write down when a colleague last saw you do it.

Go deeper

Witnessed in person, or proven by an artifact

There is a second reason the standard reads as behaviors: a behavior is startable. You can hold a 1:1 this week without first becoming a different person, and the doing quietly builds the quality everyone wanted anyway. Notice too that a witness is not always a person in the room. A calendar entry, a written feedback note, or a shared agenda all count, because a colleague could point to them later.

On your own

  1. Walk the standard and mark each line P or A: witnessed in person, or provable by an artifact like a calendar entry or a written feedback note.
  2. Pick one line you marked A and go find last month's artifact. If the search comes up empty, you have found your first honest gap.

Section 02 · The Test

Grade yourself on what they could see.

Most of us grade ourselves on intentions and grade everyone else on behavior. I meant to give feedback. I am approachable. The witness test cuts through: could a colleague have seen it happen? Run it on three ordinary claims and watch the categories separate.

Play · Behavior or intention?

Why this matters: Three claims managers make about themselves. Run the witness test on each one and call it.

Breakout · 3 min: Breakout pairs: name one thing you give yourself credit for that fails the witness test. 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Name one thing you give yourself credit for that a colleague could never have witnessed.

Go deeper

The gray zone: claims that describe a state

The trickiest claims are states: my door is open, I am available anytime. A state sounds generous and never happens at a time and place, so nobody can witness it. The conversion is always the same: pick the instance. Open door becomes office hours held every Tuesday at two. Available becomes a reply within one working day. When you hear yourself claim a state, ask for its most recent instance, with a date attached.

On your own

  1. Write three claims you would make about your own managing this year. Label each one: behavior, intention, trait, or state.
  2. Convert your weakest claim into an instance a colleague could see: a named action at a named time. Put its first occurrence on your calendar.

Section 03 · The Mirror

The mirror works when you bring evidence.

Nobody sees your answers and nothing you type is saved, so the only person you can fool here is you. An honest self-locate uses evidence: for each behavior, when did a colleague last see me do it? Then the biggest gap gets one visible move.

Practice · The honest self-locate

Why this matters: You are alone with the standard for five minutes. Choose how you rate yourself and what you do with the gap you find.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

Evidence has a shelf life

Sometimes the witness test passes on a technicality: you did hold the growth conversation, once, in the spring. So add two follow-up questions to every line you rate yourself on: when was the last instance, and how regular is the rhythm? One strong week a year ago is a memory. A modest behavior repeated monthly is what the standard is actually after, because a rhythm is what your team experiences as managing.

On your own

  1. Take the behavior you rated yourself highest on and list its last three instances with dates. If the dates take real digging, lower the rating.
  2. Give your chosen gap behavior a rhythm before you schedule it: monthly, or after each milestone. The rhythm is what makes the habit checkable.
