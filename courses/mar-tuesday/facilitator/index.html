05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If Someone says their manager never looks at Oracle: True sometimes, and the record still works: talent reviews, project staffing, and internal recruiters search it whether or not one manager does. Write for the readers you cannot see.
If Someone starts typing a real colleague's private situation into chat as an example: Redirect immediately and warmly: 'Roles, never names, and other people's private business stays unwritten, here included.' Then restate the three-shelf sort.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Who can actually see what I enter in Oracle?

Your manager and the talent teams who run reviews and staffing; each field shows its visibility if you are unsure. The working rule keeps you safe either way: write every visible entry as if a future hiring manager will read it, because the useful ones will.

What if my goal is to move away from my current team?

Mobility goals are legitimate; internal movement is exactly what the record and the Transfer Portal support. Frame the direction rather than the departure: 'grow toward project leadership' works on the record, and the specifics stay in conversations you choose to have.

I'm not a manager, so is the coaching month really for me?

Yes. Coaching is two-sided: managers are learning to ask this month, and a visible record gives them something real to ask about. Written goals make you easy to coach and easy to advocate for, whoever you report to.

Section 01 · The Why

Write the goal where readers can find it.

When a stretch project or a Transfer Portal opening appears, someone looks at the record to see who is reaching for what, and a goal that lives in your head is invisible to that search. Writing it into Oracle is how you raise your hand in advance.

The visibility rule

Managers, mentors, and internal recruiters can only work with what they can see. A goal written in Oracle stays visible in the reviews and staffing decisions you are never in the room for; a goal in your head is invisible to all of them.

In the room

Take a quick chat poll before any teaching: how many of you carry a career goal that exists nowhere in writing? Every quiet hand is a goal no talent review or staffing search can find. The readers who decide those things can only see what is written down.

Talk it through: What has kept your goals unwritten so far? And who is one reader, named by role, you would actually want to find them?

You want to move toward data work within two years. Where does that ambition do the most for you?

Grounded in: LinkedIn Workplace Learning Report: career goals and retention

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: name a goal you are carrying that exists nowhere in writing. What would change if the right person could see it?

On your own: Name a goal you are carrying that exists nowhere in writing. What would change if the right person could see it?

Go deeper

The readers you never meet

Your record has more readers than your manager: talent reviews, project leads staffing a stretch assignment, whoever hands out course seats. LinkedIn's Workplace Learning Report found that people who set career goals engage with learning about four times as much, and that development is now the first retention move organizations reach for. A written goal is what lets that machinery find you. Write for readers you have not met.

On your own

  1. Open your Oracle profile and read it as a stranger would. Note what a project lead could actually act on; it is usually less than you think.
  2. Draft one goal for a reader you have never met: the direction you want, plus a first step with a date.

Section 02 · The Sort

Some notes belong in Oracle. Some never will.

You will sit in career conversations all year: 1:1s, mentoring chats, peer feedback. Some of what you hear belongs on your visible record, some in your private notes, and some should never be written anywhere. The sort takes five seconds once you know it.

Play · System, notes, or nowhere?

Why this matters: After each conversation, you decide where the takeaway lives. Read each one and sort it.

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: one thing you would happily put on your visible record, and one thing you would keep in private notes. Compare sorts.

On your own: Write one thing you would put on your visible record and one thing you would keep in private notes.

Go deeper

The gray shelf

The three shelves get tested by gray cases. Feedback you received is yours and raw at once: hold it in private notes until you can write the pattern in your own words, then move the pattern to Oracle. Agreements that involve someone else: record your side and your commitments; theirs are theirs to write. For anything that still will not sort, one test settles it: would you be glad to have it read back to you in a year?

On your own

  1. Sort every takeaway from your last three career conversations onto a shelf: Oracle, private notes, or unwritten.
  2. Take your grayest case and run the test: would you be glad to have it read back to you in a year?
Facilitator scriptFull 11 min · Core 8

Install the three-shelf sort: visible record, private notes, or never written.

Say

“You will be in career conversations all year, your own and other people's. The sort has three shelves. Goals and agreed actions go in Oracle, visible. Raw self-observations belong in your private notes, because honesty needs a little privacy while it is fresh. Other people's private business goes nowhere, ever. Five seconds of sorting protects you and everyone around you.”

Do
  1. Run System, notes, or nowhere? live; everyone sorts on their own screen.
  2. Pause after round three; let the 'write nothing' answer breathe before you move.
  3. Run the breakout: one thing for the visible record, one for private notes.
Ask

“Why does round three, the colleague's health situation, get 'write nothing' rather than private notes?”

Expect:

  • It is theirs to tell, never mine to store
  • Notes get seen, forwarded, or found
  • Writing it down would change how I treat them

Respond: All of it. Your private notes are for your own growth. Other people's disclosures are held in trust, and trust does not have a text field.

Debrief

Yours and agreed goes visible; yours and raw stays private. Other people's private business is never yours to write.

Watch for: The quiet worry that the visible record gets used against people. Name it: the record is for growth and mobility, and section three shows how to write entries you would be glad to have read.

Transition

“You know what goes in. Now we make what goes in worth reading.”

Section 03 · The Entry

Write it so a reader can act.

A growth record is only as useful as its writing. 'Improve communication' gives a reader nothing to act on. Donald Sull's goal research at MIT Sloan is the standard here: specific and visible beats vague. Write a direction, a dated step, the agreement.

Practice · Build your entry

Why this matters: You just had a good career conversation. Capture it in two fields: the goal and the note.

Choose both steps

Grounded in: With Goals, FAST Beats SMART, Donald Sull (MIT Sloan)

Go deeper

Keep the record alive

The first entry is the easy part; records die of staleness. A goal from two roles ago reads worse than no goal at all, because it tells a reader you stopped tending the page. So sweep quarterly: retire what no longer pulls you, sharpen dates that slipped, and add the agreement from your latest career conversation. Before any career conversation, open your last note first. The follow-up line is your agenda, already written.

On your own

  1. Put a recurring fifteen-minute hold on your calendar named 'record sweep' and run the first one this week.
  2. Before your next 1:1, open your last conversation note and let its follow-up line set the first agenda item.
Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Turn goals and notes into entries a future reader can act on: direction, date, agreement, follow-up.

Say

“Oracle rewards a particular kind of writing, and it is teachable in one line: write so a reader can act. 'Improve communication' gives a reader nothing. 'Present to a leadership audience twice this year, first rep at the May ops review' gives them everything: what you want, when it starts, how to help. Donald Sull's research at MIT Sloan lands in the same place: specific goals beat 'do your best' by a wide margin, and goals work harder when other people can see them, which is the whole point of writing yours into Oracle. Same with notes. Date, agreement, follow-up, and the note runs your next conversation for you.”

Do
  1. Run Build your entry; two minutes for both fields, then save it.
  2. Ask one volunteer to read their six-months-later outcome aloud.
  3. Point at the pattern: direction plus dated step for goals; date plus agreement plus follow-up for notes.
Ask

“What makes 'improve my communication skills' so common and so useless?”

Expect:

  • It is safe and cannot be wrong
  • It came off a review form, never from a real want
  • Nobody ever taught goal-writing

Respond: Safe is the problem: it is a goal nothing can respond to. Specific goals feel exposed for a week and get fed for a year.

Debrief

Write for the reader who wants to help you. A direction, a dated first step, and notes that hold the agreement.

Watch for: Perfectionism stalling the entry. A rough goal in Oracle beats a polished one in your head, and it can be edited forever.
