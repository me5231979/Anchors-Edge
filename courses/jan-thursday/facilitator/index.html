05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If The breakout surfaces a genuinely painful change, like a reorganization in progress: Acknowledge it plainly and keep the tools pointed at it: the sort and the small step work hardest on real changes. Offer a follow-up conversation offline rather than processing it live.
If Someone challenges the premise: leadership should just communicate better: Agree without surrendering the session: better communication and personal adaptability are both real needs. This hour builds the half that is in the room's control.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Isn't this just telling us to cope with poor communication from above?

Adaptability and good communication are complements, and this session only claims the personal half. The sort even helps you ask better questions upward: a named assumption is a question leadership can actually answer, while general anxiety is much harder to respond to.

What if my experiment goes wrong and makes things worse?

That is what the two design rules protect: chore-sized and reversible. On AI experiments, the traffic light adds the safety rail: green chores only, so the worst case is a draft you discard. An experiment that cannot fail safely is scoped too big; shrink it until it can.

How do I steady a colleague when I am worried too?

Use the shape anyway; honesty is the requirement, and calm is usually the result. Naming the known and the unknown out loud steadies the speaker as much as the listener, and offering a shared step means neither of you waits alone.

Section 01 · The Sort

Ambiguity shrinks when you sort it.

Most change anxiety comes from treating one big fog as one big threat. Sorting breaks the fog into three piles: what is actually known, what is genuinely unknown, and what you have been assuming. The piles are always smaller than the fog.

The three-pile sort

When change hits, write three lists: known (confirmed by someone accountable for it), unknown (nobody has decided yet), and assumed (a guess wearing a fact's clothes). Act on the first, watch the second, test the third.

In the room

The sort works because of how uncertainty behaves. A vague threat stays the same size however long you stare at it; a named question shrinks the moment someone answers it. Sorting turns dread into named questions, and a new year that opens with AI on every agenda supplies plenty to practice on.

Talk it through: Take the line 'they are bringing in AI, which means our jobs are going away.' Which half belongs in which pile, and what is the tell?

A colleague says: 'They are bringing in AI, which means our jobs are going away.' Where does that second part belong?

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: name one workplace change on your mind, then each find one assumption you have been treating as a fact. 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Name one workplace change on your mind, then write one assumption you have been treating as a fact.

Go deeper

Sorting under pressure

The known pile has a strict entry test: someone accountable for the decision said it, in writing or somewhere they could be quoted. Secondhand certainty stays out. Assumptions are easiest to catch by their connective tissue; words like means, so, and obviously are where a fact quietly picks up a conclusion. And the sort is a living list. Re-sort when news lands, so yesterday's unknown becoming known visibly shrinks the fog.

On your own

  1. Write the three piles for the change on your mind, applying the entry test: who accountable said each known item?
  2. Circle every means and so in your own version of the worry; each one marks an assumption worth testing.

Section 02 · The Step

Trade worry for a small experiment.

Waiting for certainty feels responsible and costs you the learning season. Adaptable people take one small, reversible step that buys information: try the tool on one chore, sit in on the new process once, ask the direct question. Small steps thin the fog.

Practice · Design the experiment

Why this matters: A new AI tool is rolling out in your area, and your role will change in ways nobody has fully described. Design the step you take this week.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

Design checks for a safe experiment

A good experiment passes three design checks. It fits inside a normal week without anyone's sign-off. It leaves no mark if it fails: a discarded draft, a process you observed once, an unanswered question. And it aims at your scariest assumption first, because that is where information buys the most calm. For AI trials the traffic light is the safety rail: stay on green chores and the worst outcome is a draft you delete.

On your own

  1. Write your experiment as one sentence: this week I will take [step] to test [assumption]. If it needs sign-off, shrink it.
  2. Decide in advance what you will watch for, so the trial produces an observation instead of a feeling.
  3. Book the close-out now: one surprise and one open question, shared at your next team meeting.

Replace waiting for certainty with one small, reversible, information-buying step.

Say

“Here is the adaptable move nobody teaches: when you cannot get certainty, buy information.   2. Ask one volunteer to read their outcome aloud, weak or strong.
  3. Point at the pattern: a reversible step plus a shared close-out.
Ask

“Why does sharing one surprise with your team beat keeping the learning private?”

Expect:

  • It steadies people who have yet to try
  • It makes being mid-learning normal
  • Others correct or extend what you found

Respond: All three. One experiment shared is a team's worth of fog cleared, which is why the close-out is half the move.

Debrief

Small, reversible, this week, then shared. Experiments are how adaptable people wait.

Watch for: Experiments scoped too big to be safe or reversible. Shrink them out loud: if it cannot be undone or finished this week, it is a project, and projects need a plan.

Section 03 · The Steady Voice

Be the voice that steadies.

In a season of change, colleagues take their cue from whoever sounds grounded. Grounding has a shape: name the known, be honest about the unknown, point at one next step. Listen to three replies to the same worry and call what each does to the room.

Play · Steady or spiral?

Why this matters: A worried colleague says: 'This AI rollout is going to change everything and nobody is telling us anything.' Call what each reply does.

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: recall a change announcement that rattled your area. Which voices steadied people, and what exactly did they say? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Recall a change announcement that rattled your area. What did the steadiest voice around you actually say?

Go deeper

Steadying without overpromising

The steadying voice has one trap: comfort that hardens into a promise. 'Your job is safe' feels kind and stakes your credibility on a decision you do not own; if things shift, you become one more voice that was wrong. Steady with what you can vouch for: what was said, what you tried, what you will do next. Then close the loop later; 'here is what I found out' turns one steadying moment into a reputation for being worth asking.

On your own

  1. Script a steadying reply for a real colleague: one known you can vouch for, one honest unknown, one step together.
  2. Reread it for promises about decisions you do not own, and swap each one for something you can deliver.

Ask

“Why does 'you will be fine, just relax' fail even when it is meant kindly?”

Expect:

  • It skips the person's actual question
  • It ends the conversation
  • It sounds like the speaker knows something they do not

Respond: Right. Comfort without content reads as dismissal. The steadying reply does more work and takes only one more sentence.

Debrief

Known, unknown, one step. Honest and specific beats confident and vague, every season.

Watch for: The belief that steadying requires having answers. Stress the middle of the shape: admitting the unknown is what makes the known believable.
