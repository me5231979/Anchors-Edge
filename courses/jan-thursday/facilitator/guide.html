Audience

When things go sideways

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.

If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.

If The breakout surfaces a genuinely painful change, like a reorganization in progress: Acknowledge it plainly and keep the tools pointed at it: the sort and the small step work hardest on real changes. Offer a follow-up conversation offline rather than processing it live.

If Someone challenges the premise: leadership should just communicate better: Agree without surrendering the session: better communication and personal adaptability are both real needs. This hour builds the half that is in the room's control.

Tough questions, ready answers

Q: Isn't this just telling us to cope with poor communication from above?
Adaptability and good communication are complements, and this session only claims the personal half. The sort even helps you ask better questions upward: a named assumption is a question leadership can actually answer, while general anxiety is much harder to respond to.

Q: What if my experiment goes wrong and makes things worse?
That is what the two design rules protect: chore-sized and reversible. On AI experiments, the traffic light adds the safety rail: green chores only, so the worst case is a draft you discard. An experiment that cannot fail safely is scoped too big; shrink it until it can.

Q: How do I steady a colleague when I am worried too?
Use the shape anyway; honesty is the requirement, and calm is usually the result. Naming the known and the unknown out loud steadies the speaker as much as the listener, and offering a shared step means neither of you waits alone.

Known, unknown, assumed Full 8 min · Core 6

Teach the three-pile sort that shrinks ambiguity into actionable pieces.

Say

“Here is the shorthand for every change season: the fog is never as big as it feels. Most of what makes ambiguity heavy is that it arrives unsorted. So the first adaptability skill is a sorting move. Three piles: known, meaning confirmed by someone accountable for it; unknown, meaning genuinely undecided; and assumed, which is a guess wearing a fact's clothes. The piles are always smaller than the fog.”

Do

  1. Read the concept card aloud, once, slowly, then let the context on screen land; the live room gets the framing, no quiz.
  2. Run the discussion on the colleague's line: which half is known, which half is assumed, and what is the tell?
  3. Run the breakout: one assumption dressed up as a fact, 90 seconds each way.

Ask

“Think of a change on your own plate. What is one assumption you have been treating as a fact?”

Expect:

  • Job or role fears stated as certainties
  • Beliefs about what leadership has decided
  • Timelines nobody actually announced

Respond: Notice how each one turns into a question the moment it is named. Assumptions are only dangerous while they stay disguised.

Debrief: Act on the known, watch the unknown, test the assumed. That single sort is most of what calm people are doing.

Watch for: The sort turning into a gripe session about specific leaders or colleagues. Keep it on situations and roles, never names, and steer each pile toward testable statements.

Transition: “The sort tells you where the fog is. The next skill walks into it.”

Core path: Core: read the card, run the discussion from chat, ask for one assumption.

The small experiment Full 10 min · Core 7

Replace waiting for certainty with one small, reversible, information-buying step.

Say

“Here is the adaptable move nobody teaches: when you cannot get certainty, buy information. A small experiment is one reversible, chore-sized step taken this week: try the new tool on one task, sit in on the new process once, ask the direct question. This month the obvious experiment is an AI one, and Tuesday's traffic light keeps it safe: green chores, public or generic, are the perfect testing ground.”

Do

  1. Run Design the experiment; two minutes to make both choices and run it.
  2. Ask one volunteer to read their outcome aloud, weak or strong.
  3. Point at the pattern: a reversible step plus a shared close-out.

Ask

“Why does sharing one surprise with your team beat keeping the learning private?”

Expect:

  • It steadies people who have yet to try
  • It makes being mid-learning normal
  • Others correct or extend what you found

Respond: All three. One experiment shared is a team's worth of fog cleared, which is why the close-out is half the move.

Debrief: Small, reversible, this week, then shared. Experiments are how adaptable people wait.

Watch for: Experiments scoped too big to be safe or reversible. Shrink them out loud: if it cannot be undone or finished this week, it is a project, and projects need a plan.

Transition: “You can steady yourself now. The last skill steadies the people around you.”

Core path: Core: everyone runs the builder solo; skip the read-aloud.

Steady the room Full 9 min · Core 6

Give learners the steadying reply: name the known, admit the unknown, offer one step.

Say

“Every changing workplace has two contagious moods, spiral and steady, and whichever one speaks most confidently spreads. Steadying has a learnable shape: name what is actually known, admit what is genuinely unknown, and offer one small step, ideally together. You are about to hear three replies to the same worried colleague. Call what each one does to the room.”

Do

  1. Run Steady or spiral? live; everyone plays on their own screen.
  2. After the dismissive reply, pause: ask why kindness landed as shutdown.
  3. Close with the shape typed in chat: known, unknown, one step.

Ask

“Why does 'you will be fine, just relax' fail even when it is meant kindly?”

Expect:

  • It skips the person's actual question
  • It ends the conversation
  • It sounds like the speaker knows something they do not

Respond: Right. Comfort without content reads as dismissal. The steadying reply does more work and takes only one more sentence.

Debrief: Known, unknown, one step. Honest and specific beats confident and vague, every season.

Watch for: The belief that steadying requires having answers. Stress the middle of the shape: admitting the unknown is what makes the known believable.

Transition: “Quick recap, then you build your own card.”

Core path: Core: run the trainer, skip the pause, take one voice on the debrief.

Recap quiz Full 4 min · Core 3
