05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If Someone asks a Portal or Marketplace mechanics question beyond today's scope: Park it visibly: 'Good question, logging it for the program team.' Point them to the Transfer Portal month materials in the series library and send parked questions upward with the pulse.
If A participant says their manager punishes people for looking internally: Take it seriously and keep it roles-only: 'If that is happening, there are channels for it, and I will point you to them after.' Restate the policy plainly; never litigate a specific manager on the call.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Will my manager be notified if I update my profile or get matched?

Your profile is yours, and keeping it current is normal professional upkeep, expected of everyone this month. Best practice is to bring your named direction to your manager yourself; a manager who knows your direction can advocate for it.

Is this just a way to move people into jobs nobody wants?

The matching starts from your skills and your named direction, and you can always say no. Surfacing is an offer; nothing about these systems drafts anyone anywhere.

I have been in the same role for fifteen years. Is this really for me?

Especially for you. Long tenure usually means a deep skill set and a thin profile. The skills sweep is where fifteen years of capability finally gets written down, and stretch projects surface for exactly that depth.

Section 01 · The Follow-Up

Point the tools at your own career.

Earlier in the series you learned the Transfer Portal as a system. Today it gets personal. The Talent Marketplace holds your skills profile and surfaces growth work; the Portal carries internal moves. Together they are a planning kit for what you do next.

The planning kit

Read the two systems as one kit. The Talent Marketplace grows you where you are: skills profile, stretch projects, growth work. The Transfer Portal moves you when you are ready: internal roles across Vanderbilt. A real plan uses both.

In the room

Everyone in this room already knows the Transfer Portal; the series taught it in depth. What most people have never done is sit down with the Marketplace and the Portal open and ask the selfish question: what is my plan? Today is that hour, with the university's explicit blessing.

Talk it through: When you learned the Portal earlier in the series, what did you privately wonder about your own next move? Say the honest version out loud.

Quick check: you want a growth plan, not just a job search. Where does it start?

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: what do you want these systems working on for you this year, a skill, a project, a move? One pair shares.

On your own: Write down what you want these systems working on for you this year: a skill, a project, or a move.

Go deeper

Growth first, move second

The commonest planning mistake is starting with a destination role and working backward. Roles change, get frozen, or go to someone readier. Directions are sturdier than destinations: name the kind of work and the skills it runs on, then let the Marketplace grow you toward it while the Portal watches for openings shaped like it. When a role finally surfaces, you are applying from readiness instead of hope.

On your own

  1. Write your direction as one sentence: the kind of work, not a job title. Test it: would three different real roles fit inside it?
  2. List the two skills that direction runs on which you cannot evidence yet. Those are your Marketplace growth targets.
  3. Open the Transfer Portal once this week and note which current openings rhyme with your direction, even ones you are not ready for.

Section 02 · The Readiness

Findable is a choice.

Matching is only as good as what it can read. Two moves keep you findable: a Talent Marketplace profile that says what you can actually do, and a direction named in writing. Build both here.

Practice · Build your findability

Why this matters: Two entries decide what the matching can see. Choose how you write your skills and how you name your direction.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

The evidence line

A skill without an evidence line is a keyword, and keywords are cheap. The strongest lines have three parts: what you did, the scale or the stakes, and what it produced. 'Process mapping: redesigned intake during our busiest cycle and cleared the backlog' is credible, findable, and easy for an advocate to repeat. Skills from past roles count in full; the matching has no way to discount where a capability came from.

On your own

  1. Take your three strongest skills and write the three-part evidence line for each: the thing, the stakes, the result.
  2. Add one skill from a past role or outside work that your current job never uses, with its own evidence line.
  3. Read each line as a stranger. If it could describe anyone with your title, sharpen it until it could only describe you.

Section 03 · The Culture

An internal move is a career path.

The systems only work if people believe using them is safe. So hear the policy out loud: internal mobility is a normal, encouraged career path at Vanderbilt. Keeping talent inside the university is the point of Goal 1. Some old myths need retiring first.

Play · Myth or normal?

Why this matters: How people talk about internal moves decides whether anyone makes them. Call each one; the systems live or die inside that culture.

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: what have you heard, in any workplace, about what happens to people who apply internally? Myth or real, 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Write down one thing you have believed about applying internally. After this section, decide: myth or real?

Go deeper

The manager conversation, scripted

The myths mostly die in one specific conversation: telling your manager you are looking at a move. Plan it in three beats. The direction: what you are growing toward and why. The timing: nothing is decided, you are being deliberate, not restless. The continuity: what you would do to land your current work well. Managers who hear all three usually turn into advocates, because you have just shown them exactly how to support you.

On your own

  1. Draft the three beats for your own manager conversation: direction, timing, continuity. Two sentences each.
  2. Say the direction beat out loud once. If it sounds like an apology, rewrite it until it sounds like a plan.
Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Retire the loyalty myths and state internal mobility as a normal, encouraged Vanderbilt career path.

Say

“None of the tooling matters if people believe using it is dangerous. So hear it stated plainly: internal mobility is a normal, encouraged career path at Vanderbilt. A move across the university keeps your talent, your relationships, and your institutional knowledge here. The alternative to an internal move is usually an external one, and everybody loses that trade.”

Do
  1. Run Myth or normal? live; everyone plays on their own screen.
  2. After round one, pause: ask who has heard that myth somewhere, show of hands or chat.
  3. Run the breakout on what people have heard about applying internally.
Ask

“What would make you hesitate to make an internal move, honestly?”

Expect:

  • My manager's reaction
  • Feeling disloyal to my team
  • Not feeling qualified enough

Respond: Name each one back to the room. The manager fear is the myth we just retired; the qualification fear is why matching runs on skills, never titles.

Debrief

Myths punish ambition, and they only survive in silence. Vanderbilt is saying the opposite out loud: move, grow, stay.

Watch for: Anyone hearing this as a promise of promotions. The commitment is visibility of opportunity and safety to pursue it, never a guaranteed yes.
