Begin the session

Section 01 · One Skill

Pick a skill you can finish.

The best 90-day skill goal is small and lives inside work you already do, what ATD calls learning in the flow of work. Prompting a solid first draft of your weekly report beats mastering AI.

The 90-day skill test

A good 90-day skill passes three questions: can I name the work I will use it on next week, can someone tell in September whether I built it, and can I practice it inside my actual job? If any answer is no, shrink the skill.

In the room

In the room: bring to mind the AI skill you keep meaning to build, in the ambitious form it lives in your head. Run it through the card's three questions: named work, checkable by September, practiced inside your job. Most ambitions fail the second question, and shrinking is what rescues them.

Talk it through: Which of the three questions kills the most skill goals around here: named work, checkable, or inside the job? Why that one?

Quick check: which of these is the strongest 90-day skill goal?

Grounded in: Learning in the flow of work, four keys to success (ATD)

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: trade one skill you could realistically build by September. Partner's job: shrink it until it honestly fits 90 days. 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Write one skill you could realistically build by September. Then shrink it until it honestly fits 90 days.

Go deeper

Shrinking without settling

The shrink can go too far: a skill so small it was really a task. The test is repetition. A task happens once; a skill changes how a category of work goes. Drafting one newsletter with AI is a task; prompting a solid first draft of any recurring update is a skill sized for 90 days. Keep the ambition as your direction and let the 90-day version be its first checkable installment. The next installment gets its own 90 days.

On your own

  1. Write your skill three ways: the ambitious version, the shrunken version, the too-small version. Mark where the skill stops and the task begins.
  2. Name the recurring work your shrunken skill upgrades, then put the first practice rep on your calendar this week.

Section 02 · One Task

Hand AI a task, once the light turns green.

The plan's second line is a task you stop doing by hand. For your plan, pick a green task: public or generic, go. The full light still governs the rest: yellow is internal, approved VU tools only; red is private information about people, never.

Play · Clear the handoff

Why this matters: Three tasks people wanted to hand to AI this year. Run each through the traffic light before anything moves.

Breakout · 3 min: Breakout pairs: name the task you want to hand to AI and run it through the light out loud. Partner checks the call. 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Name the task you want to hand to AI and run it through the light. Write the color next to it.

Go deeper

The light on compound tasks

Most real tasks are compound, and one red ingredient colors the whole thing. An event write-up is green until the paragraph about a colleague's departure goes in; meeting notes are yellow until they record who struggled with what. Run the light on the task's ingredients, not its title. Often you can split it: hand AI the generic frame, with an approved VU tool as your default, and keep the sentences about people yours.

On your own

  1. List your chosen handoff task's ingredients: each kind of information that goes into it. Color every ingredient, not just the task.
  2. If any ingredient runs yellow or red, split the task: write the green half as your plan's handoff line and keep the rest by hand.

Section 03 · One Habit

Choose what stays yours.

The third line is a quiet promise: one habit you keep doing yourself, on purpose. Maybe it is the hard message you draft alone, because drafting is how you think. Naming what stays human keeps you the one steering.

Practice · Assemble the plan

Why this matters: A plan survives on how it is written and how it is reviewed. Choose both.

Choose both steps

Grounded in: If-then planning, Heidi Grant, Harvard Business Review (2014)

Go deeper

Why the habit line holds the plan

The habit line looks decorative and does the most work. People who cannot say what they keep swing between refusing AI entirely and drifting into handoffs they never chose. A named habit is the anchor that makes every other handoff deliberate. Choose one where the doing changes you, like the hard message you draft alone because drafting is how you think. If losing the habit would cost you nothing, pick a truer one.

On your own

  1. Write why your habit stays human in one sentence that names what the doing gives you, not what AI would get wrong.
  2. Do the habit once this week, then check whether your sentence held up. If it did not, choose a truer habit.

The core idea

Write the three lines, date each one, and book the day-30 review with a witness.

The recap, with the answers

1 · What makes a skill goal fit a 90-day plan?

Answer: It is named, checkable, and practiced in your real work

Small, named, and checkable beats broad and inspiring. If September cannot verify it, shrink it.

2 · You want AI to summarize an internal planning doc for your team meeting. The light says...

Answer: Yellow: approved VU tools only

Internal content is yellow: fine inside approved VU tools. Green is public or generic; red is private information about people, never.

3 · What is the strongest way to keep a 90-day plan alive?

Answer: A day-30 review with a colleague who has your plan

An early checkpoint plus a witness catches a stalled plan while there is still time to restart it.

Your commitment

Write your three lines: the skill, the green-light handoff, the human habit. Keep everything task level, no names, no private details. Nothing you type is saved or sent.

  • Start the skill. Book the first practice rep this week: the named task, the approved tool, thirty minutes on the calendar.
  • Make the handoff. Confirm the task is green, then do the first AI-assisted version this week and compare it honestly with your usual work.
  • Guard the habit. Do the human habit once this week, on purpose, and notice what it gives you that automation would miss.
