05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If The room turns the session into complaints about a specific vendor or product: Steer back to the pattern: no verdicts on named products here. The filter is the takeaway, and it outlives any one tool. Keep the examples generic.
If Someone argues the beneficiary test makes them distrust colleagues: Reframe warmly: the test is about incentives, never character. A colleague can be sincere and wrong at the same time; the evidence test catches that, and the beneficiary test simply asks who is selling.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Isn't this just teaching us to be cynical about AI?

The filter fails hype and passes value, and it runs in both directions: dismissing a genuinely useful tool is exactly as unfiltered as buying the demo. The colleague claim in the trainer passes cleanly. Skepticism that names its own mind-changer is called judgment.

What if leadership has already decided to adopt a tool the filter would fail?

The filter still helps: it tells you what evidence to gather during rollout. Name the mind-changer, run your own tasks through the tool, and report what you find. Honest evidence from real use is valuable to leadership in both directions, and Goal 3 explicitly asks for it.

Where does the traffic light fit into testing new tools?

Every test rides the light. A trial uses public or generic content freely; internal content only inside approved VU tools; and private information about people never goes into a test, no matter how promising the tool. An unapproved tool gets tested with green content only, if at all.

Section 01 · The Filter

Ask three questions of every claim.

The filter is three questions, asked fast. What is the evidence, and would it survive someone checking? Who benefits from my believing this? And what would change my mind, in either direction? A claim that survives all three has earned some trust.

The filter

Three tests, one minute. Evidence: what is it, would it survive checking? Beneficiary: who gains if I believe this? Mind-changer: what result would move me? No possible mind-changer means it is a belief, and beliefs resist evidence.

In the room

The filter runs on your enthusiasms as much as your doubts. The claims most worth testing are the ones you want to be true: the tool that would fix your week, the headline that flatters your skills. Run the tests fastest there: from the inside, a wish and a well-evidenced claim feel identical.

Talk it through: Share one AI claim you half believe right now. Which of the three tests would it wobble on first, and what result would honestly change your mind?

Quick check: why does the mind-changer test matter?

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: recall one AI claim you heard this month, from anywhere. Run it through the three tests together. One pair shares.

On your own: Recall one AI claim you heard this month and run it through the three tests. Write down where it wobbled.

Go deeper

The mind-changer, written down

A mind-changer works best written, and it needs a size. 'Good results' moves nobody; 'drafts a usable first version of the Thursday summary in under ten minutes' is a bar a claim can visibly clear or miss. Write one in each direction: the result that would win you over and the result that would end your enthusiasm. If you can only write one of the two, you have just found the side you are biased toward.

On your own

  1. Take one claim about AI and your work that you believe. Write the specific result that would make you drop it.
  2. Take one claim you dismiss and write the result that would win you over. Notice which sentence was harder.

Install the filter: evidence, beneficiary, mind-changer, one minute per claim.

Say

“This week your manager mapped exposure and Tuesday you sorted your own tasks. Today is the quality control for all of it, because every map and every sort rests on claims about what AI can do. Three questions, one minute: what is the evidence, who benefits from my believing this, and what would change my mind?”

Do
  1. Read the three tests slowly, once each, then bring up the In the room card.
  2. Run the discussion prompt: one half-believed claim and the test it wobbles on. Take two or three voices.
  3. Run the breakout: one recent AI claim through all three tests.
Ask

“Which of the three tests do you skip most often?”

Expect:

  • Most say the mind-changer
  • Some admit the beneficiary test feels cynical
  • A few say they rarely see the evidence at all

Respond: The mind-changer is the one that separates reasoning from believing, and it is the cheapest: one honest sentence before you decide.

Debrief

The filter is a habit, and habits need to be fast: three questions before a claim gets your belief, whichever direction it leans. Enthusiasm and dismissal both get filtered.

Watch for: People aiming the filter only at claims they already dislike. The tests work on your favorite claims too, and that is where they earn the most.

Transition

“The tests are simple. Hearing when to reach for them takes a tuned ear.”

Section 02 · The Patterns

Learn the tells hype repeats.

Hype repeats itself: a giant number with no baseline, urgency that never survives a second look, a seller quoted as the judge. Value talks about a specific task and invites you to check. Practice on real claims until those patterns are quick to spot.

Play · Hype or value?

Why this matters: Run the filter on each claim and note which tell decides your verdict. Getting the middle case right matters most: unclear claims earn a test, never free belief.

Grounded in: The AI Index Report, Stanford HAI

Breakout · 3 min: Breakout pairs: one of you pitches a tool with pure hype, the other runs the filter out loud. 90 seconds, then swap.

On your own: Find one AI claim in your inbox or feed right now. Label it hype, value, or unproven, and note which tell decided it.

Go deeper

Hype does its best work secondhand

By the time a vendor's number reaches you through a colleague, the tells have worn off: no byline, no ticking clock, just a trusted voice saying everyone is doing this now. So filter the claim and spare the messenger. Ask where the number started and whether anyone in the chain saw the before and after. For claims about what AI can actually do, Stanford's yearly AI Index is a scoreboard with no product behind it.

On your own

  1. Recall one claim that reached you through someone you trust. Trace it: where did the number start, and who along the way verified it?
  2. Rehearse the trace-back line: 'That sounds useful. Where did the number come from?' Practice until it sounds like plain curiosity.

Say

“Hype is lazy; it repeats itself. Giant numbers with no baseline. Urgency that evaporates when you check the calendar. Sellers quoted as judges of their own product. Do

  2. After the colleague claim, pause: ask what makes it so much more credible than the vendor's.
  3. Run the breakout: one pitches pure hype, one filters out loud, then swap.
Ask

“Why does the colleague's claim pass the beneficiary test so cleanly?”

Expect:

  • They gain nothing from your belief
  • They offered the before and after
  • The task is specific and close to yours

Respond: Right. And notice that the offer to show the work is itself a tell: value invites checking, hype discourages it.

Debrief

Hype stacks its tells: big, vague, urgent, interested. Value is specific, checkable, and calm. The unclear middle deserves a cheap test before belief.

Watch for: Cynics using the session to dismiss every AI claim. The filter passes real value too; the colleague claim exists so the room feels the difference.

“A tuned ear is good. A decision method is better.”

Section 03 · The Move

Point the filter at a real decision.

Most claims arrive as pressure to adopt. Forbes reported MIT's finding that about 95 percent of enterprise AI pilots showed no measurable return, so answer a claim with a small, cheap test on your own work, mind-changer named before it runs.

Practice · Design the deciding test

Why this matters: A tool claim has reached your team and people are excited. Choose your evidence and your bar.

Choose both steps

Grounded in: Why 95 percent of AI pilots fail, Andrea Hill (Forbes)

Go deeper

A bar you can actually judge

The weak point of most trials is the missing baseline. Before the tool touches anything, time the task the way you do it now and keep one recent output as the comparison copy. Then write the bar where the team can see it, with a number and a quality condition. Decide in advance what a near miss means: a tool that almost clears the bar earns one revision of the test, and only one, or the trial quietly becomes a subscription.

On your own

  1. Pick the recurring task your team would trial first. Time it once this week and save one output as the baseline copy.
  2. Write the bar as one sentence with a number and a quality condition, and share it before any demo runs.
Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Turn the filter into a decision method: own-work evidence and a mind-changer named in advance.

Say

“Here is where the filter stops being commentary and starts making decisions. When a tool claim reaches your team, the answer is rarely an instant yes or no. It is a small test on your own real work, with the bar named before the test runs: it wins if it saves an hour with no accuracy loss. Set the bar first and the result settles it, whichever way it lands.”

Do
  1. Run Design the deciding test; give the room two minutes for both choices.
  2. Ask one volunteer to read a weak outcome aloud; let the room name the missing test.
  3. Point at the pattern: own-work evidence, bar set in advance.
Ask

“Why does naming the mind-changer before the trial change the whole decision?”

Expect:

  • It stops both sides claiming victory
  • It makes the trial evidence instead of theater
  • It protects the decision from the loudest voice

Respond: All three. A bar set in advance is the cheapest honesty mechanism a team can buy, and it works on far more than AI tools.

Debrief

The deciding test: one real recurring task, an approved VU tool, output checked against today's way, and the winning condition written down first.

Watch for: People designing trials on vendor sample data. Redirect to their own recurring tasks; borrowed data proves nothing about their week.

A decision the whole team can trust. The test was real, the bar was public, and the result settled it either way.

The recap, with the answers

1 · The three tests of the filter are...
