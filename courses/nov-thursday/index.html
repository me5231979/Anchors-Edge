Section 01 · The Reframe

Lead with a real question.

Networking feels bad when it is extraction, contacts collected for use someday. The alternative is curiosity about how other work happens: enrollment forecasts, or what facilities sees at 5am. A question you genuinely want answered makes the outreach honest.

The curiosity test

Before reaching out, find the question you actually want answered about their work. If you have one, the outreach will feel natural to send and to receive. If you cannot find one, you picked the wrong person, and no script will fix it.

In the room

The ick is worth trusting: it is what extraction feels like from the outside. Curiosity is the honest replacement, and last month primed it. Seeing the talent around you means you already know whose work makes you wonder, and a real question is the only script this ever needs.

Talk it through: When has an outreach felt gross to receive, and what gave it away? What would the genuinely curious version of that same message have sounded like?

What separates connecting from the networking that feels gross?

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: name one Vanderbilt team whose work you are genuinely curious about, and the question you would ask them. 90 seconds each.

On your own: Name one Vanderbilt team you are genuinely curious about, and the question you would ask them.

Go deeper

When the curiosity will not come

Curiosity can be primed. Skim the university news page, reread your department's last newsletter, or chase the last process that mystified you: who decides that, and how do they know? The question you want survives a simple test: you would still ask it if nothing ever came of the coffee. If a person interests you only for their influence, the test failed; start from the work you wonder about.

On your own

  1. Write down three Vanderbilt processes that genuinely mystify you, and the team that owns each one.
  2. For one of them, draft the question you would ask even if the coffee never led anywhere else.

Section 02 · The Coffee

Run the 20-minute coffee.

The 20-minute virtual coffee is the unit of internal networking. Stanford's weak ties experiment found the useful leads sit one ring outside your circle, so invite someone from there. Build the ask.

Go deeper

Three questions that carry the meeting

The best coffee questions share a shape: they ask how the work happens. 'What surprised you about the orientation rebuild?' opens stories; 'any openings coming up?' closes them. Prepare three in advance, then let the second and third bend toward whatever the first answer surprised you with. Prepared and rigid are different things, and the bend is where the conversation gets good.

On your own

  1. Draft the three questions for the coffee on your capstone card. Test each one: is it about their work?
  2. Plan the close: the thank-you plus the one specific detail you want to remember from their answers.

Section 03 · The Warmth

Follow up small and specific.

Connections go quiet without contact, though the dormant-ties research finds the trust survives long silences. A warm follow-up is specific, asks for nothing, and takes two minutes. This section covers when to send one and what it says.

Play · Call the follow-up

Why this matters: Read each move and call it: warm, transactional, or too much. A warm follow-up is specific to them and asks for nothing.

Grounded in: The power of reconnection, dormant ties (MIT Sloan Review)

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: name one contact in another department you could send a two-minute warm follow-up to this week. What would it say?

On your own: Name one contact in another department and the two-minute warm follow-up you could send this week.

Go deeper

A cadence you will actually keep

The follow-up habit dies when it turns into homework. Attach it to moments instead of a schedule: you read something and think of them, their team ships something visible, a conversation echoes a thing they told you. Two minutes at the moment of thinking beats any reminder to be thoughtful later. A handful of bridges kept this way carries more opportunity than a hundred contacts kept cold.

On your own

  1. List the internal contacts you would call bridges today, and note when each one last heard from you.
  2. Pick the coldest bridge still worth keeping and send its two-minute reopener before you close this tab.
