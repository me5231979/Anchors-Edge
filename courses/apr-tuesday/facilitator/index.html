05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If Someone asks which specific AI tools are approved right now: Point to the approved VU tools list on the Anchor's Edge dashboard rather than reciting from memory; the list moves, the traffic light does not.
If Someone insists their whole job should be left alone: Agree that plenty of it may be, then ask for their most repetitive task and sort just that one together. One honest call usually unlocks the rest.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

If I automate parts of my job, am I automating myself out of it?

The sort is per task, and no job is one task. Staff who can decompose, sort, and check their own work are exactly what Goal 3 calls workforce readiness; the freed hours move to the augment and leave-alone work where your judgment shows. The risk sits with unsorted weeks, and yours is sorted.

My manager hasn't said anything about AI. Should I wait?

Sorting your own week needs nobody's permission, and testing inside approved VU tools on green and yellow work is already within the rules. Share your sort with your manager afterward; Monday's session gave them a team-level version of this exact map, so you will be speaking the same language.

How do I know if a task is yellow or red?

Ask what the content is about. Internal but institutional, like meeting notes or draft plans: yellow, approved VU tools only. About an identifiable person, their health, performance, or circumstances: red, never. When a task mixes both, the red parts come out before any tool sees it, or the whole task stays human.

Section 01 · The Breakdown

A week is just tasks wearing a blur.

Asked what you do, you say your title. Asked what you did Tuesday, you list tasks: compiled the report, answered the queue, drafted the agenda. The sort only works at that second level, so the first skill is writing your week the way Tuesday actually went.

The decomposition rule

A sortable task names an output and a rhythm: the thing produced and how often. 'Communications' is a job. 'Draft the Thursday newsletter from submitted items' is a task, and a task can be sorted.

In the room

When work stays at the altitude of titles and projects, AI talk turns into dread about whole jobs. Nobody automates 'coordinator'; tools meet the Monday summary, the intake queue, the standard reply. Get your week written at that level and the fear turns into decisions you can actually make.

Talk it through: Describe last Tuesday to your neighbor as three tasks. Which one was hardest to name, and why had it gone invisible?

Quick check: which of these is ready to sort?

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: turn 'my job' into three real tasks from this week, specific enough that someone else could picture doing them. Swap lists.

On your own: Write three real tasks from this week, specific enough that someone else could picture doing them.

Go deeper

The invisible half of your week

The fastest way past the blur is evidence. Your calendar and your sent folder remember Tuesday better than you do: standing meetings carry prep work, and the sent folder shows the same three replies leaving every week. Most people find a chunk of their week is repeating work they never named. That unnamed work goes to the top of the sort list, because unexamined routine is exactly what AI handles best.

On your own

  1. Open last week's calendar and sent folder. List every repeating piece of work you find, written as an output and a rhythm.
  2. Star the tasks you never think of as tasks: the prep, the formatting, the standard replies. Sort those first.

Section 02 · The Sort

Automate, augment, or leave alone.

Three calls, made per task. Automate: AI does it, you check the output. Augment: AI helps, you drive. Leave alone: human all the way, because the task runs on trust, judgment, or private information about people. The traffic light rides along on every call.

Play · Make the call

Why this matters: Three tasks from real Vanderbilt weeks. Sort each one.

Breakout · 3 min: Breakout pairs: each pick one task from your list and make the call out loud. Partner gets one challenge question. 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Pick one task from your list and make the call: automate, augment, or leave alone. Write one line on why.

Go deeper

The mixed task and the red thread

Calls get interesting when one task mixes colors. A weekly update that quotes a colleague's medical situation is an augment task carrying a red thread, and the red thread wins: the private parts come out before any tool sees the rest, or the whole task stays human. The quieter skill is naming your leave-alone pile out loud. That pile is the case for what you uniquely bring, and it belongs in your next career conversation.

On your own

  1. Sort ten tasks from your week into the three buckets. Note which bucket filled up and whether that surprised you.
  2. Find one task with a red thread: sortable overall, private people details inside. Write which parts come out before a tool touches it.
  3. Write two lines on your leave-alone pile and what it says about the value you bring.

And every call rides the traffic light we share all year: green is public or generic, go; yellow is internal, approved VU tools only; red is private information about people, never.”

Ask

Expect:

  • Many guess automate is small
  • Some fear the leave-alone pile is shrinking
  • A few will say they have no idea yet

Respond: Most weeks sort mostly into augment, and the leave-alone pile is where your distinctly human value lives. Naming it is worth as much as the automation.

Debrief

Fixed format on public content leans automate. Internal drafting leans augment in approved VU tools. Trust, judgment, and people data stay human.

Watch for: Anyone treating leave alone as failure or the lesser bucket. Reframe it: that pile is the case for the human in the role.

Section 03 · The Move

One call earns a test drive.

A sort that stays theoretical changes nothing. Pick one automate or augment call and run it once this week: one task, one date, one approved VU tool, and a checking step you actually perform. The test either saves you an hour or teaches you why it can't yet.

Practice · Set up the test

Why this matters: Your sort is only as good as its first real run. Choose the task and the checking step.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

Read the test like evidence

A test deserves a fair reading. Count the whole cost: setup, prompting, and checking time against the old way, because a tool that saves forty minutes and needs thirty of checking earned ten. What your check caught becomes the permanent checklist for the task. A failed test is a real result too: write one line on why the call was wrong, then re-sort the task. Scale to a second task after two clean runs of the first.

On your own

  1. After the test, write three numbers: minutes the old way, minutes with the tool including the check, and errors the check caught.
  2. Turn what the check caught into a named checklist you run every time the task ships.
Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Say

“A sort that lives in your head is a nice opinion. It becomes a redesign of your week the first time you test a call: one task, one date, one approved tool, and a checking step with names on it. We are going to build that test now, and the checking step is the part people skip and regret.”

Do

Ask

“Why does the checking step stay even when the tool has been right ten times in a row?”

Expect:

  • The eleventh time is the one that ships wrong
  • The check is what makes automate safe
  • The output still carries your name

Respond: Right. Automate never means unattended. The output ships under your name, so the check is part of the task, permanently.

Debrief

The test formula: one task, one date, one approved tool, one named check. Small enough to run, real enough to teach.

Watch for: Big-bang testers trying to automate a whole workflow at once. Shrink it: one task, this week.
