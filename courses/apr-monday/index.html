Manager Monday · The manager lens · Spot AI-Exposed Work

Map the work before it moves.

AI is already reshaping some of the work your team repeats every week. This session gives you a manager's map of it: which tasks are exposed, which stay human, and how to talk about the difference so your team sees the change before it sees them.

By the end of this session, you will be able to

  1. Map your team's recurring work as tasks, separate from the people who hold them
  2. Sort each task honestly by AI exposure: high, partial, or low
  3. Open one team conversation that frames exposure as redesign the team helps write

Section 01 · The Map

AI meets your team one task at a time.

Nobody's whole job is exposed to AI; pieces of it are. The map starts with a plain list of your team's recurring tasks: the weekly report, the intake queue, the scheduling. Write tasks, hold the people out of it, and the picture gets honest fast.

The mapping rule

Map the work, never the people. List each recurring task on its own line: what gets produced, how often, from what inputs. A task-level map lets you redesign work without putting anyone's job on trial.

In the room

Map this work by role and the month becomes a quiet argument about whose job is safe, with honesty as the first casualty. Map it by task and the same team can study the same work in the open. A task on the board puts nobody on trial, so people tell the truth about what the week is made of.

Talk it through: Name one task your team repeats weekly, in task language: output, rhythm, inputs. What gets easier to say once the name is off it?

Quick check: which entry belongs on an AI-exposure map?

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: name two recurring tasks your team does every week, in task language, no names attached. One pair shares their list.

On your own: List five recurring tasks your team does every week, in task language, no names attached.

Go deeper

Write the task like a recipe

A sortable task line has three parts: the output, the rhythm, and the inputs. 'Board report, monthly, from three spreadsheets' sorts in seconds; 'reporting' never will. The entries worth hunting are the invisible ones: work living in one person's head, or the cleanup nobody calls a task. Unlisted tasks are often the most exposed, because nobody has ever looked at them straight.

On your own

  1. Rewrite one recurring task as output, rhythm, inputs. If a colleague could picture doing it from your line, it is ready to sort.
  2. Add two invisible tasks to your map: work only one person knows, or cleanup nobody names. Write them in the same format.
  3. Keep the list where you plan your week and add one task line a day until you reach ten.

Section 02 · The Sort

Sort it straight, without flattery or panic.

Two lies wreck an exposure map: everything is exposed, so panic, and nothing is, so relax. The truth is a sort. High exposure means the task could largely run through AI. Partial means AI helps while a person drives. Low means it stays human.

Play · Call the exposure

Why this matters: Three tasks from real Vanderbilt weeks. Read each one and make the exposure call.

Breakout · 3 min: Breakout pairs: pick one task from your list and call its exposure. Does your partner agree? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Pick one task from your list and call its exposure: high, partial, or low. Write one line on why.

Go deeper

When a task refuses to sort

Some tasks resist a single call because they are bundles. The monthly newsletter is drafting from public content, choosing the lead story, and replying to an upset reader, and each piece sorts differently. When a call feels impossible, split the task into steps and sort the steps; the argument usually dissolves. A sort is also a snapshot: tools improve, so a low call today deserves a fresh look next quarter.

On your own

  1. Take the task that was hardest to call. Break it into three or four steps and give each step its own exposure call.
  2. Mark any step touching private information about people. Those stay human, whatever you decide about the rest of the bundle.
  3. Date the sort and set a reminder to rerun it in a quarter.

Section 03 · The Move

Bring the map to the team.

A map you keep in a drawer breeds rumor. Shared early, it becomes a redesign the team helps write: what gets automated, what gets augmented, where the freed hours go. How you open that conversation decides whether it lands as invitation or verdict.

Practice · Open the team conversation

Why this matters: Your team will hear about AI exposure from someone. Choose how they hear it from you.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

Keep the map alive after the talk

The opening meeting is the easy part; the promise gets kept in what happens next. Put the map where the team can see and edit it, and treat every task they add as a gift. Then keep the redesign promise visibly the first time it is tested: when the first task moves to a tool, let the team decide in the open what fills the freed hour. One kept promise buys more trust than a season of reassurance.

On your own

  1. Draft the two sentences you will open with: the invitation to sort the map together and the freed-hours promise. Say them aloud once before the meeting.
  2. Choose where the shared map will live and add a recurring slot on the team agenda to revisit it.

The core idea

Exposure you can see becomes a redesign. Exposure you ignore arrives as a surprise.
