Begin the session

Section 01 · The Streams

The standard is measured in behavior.

The manager standard is a list of behaviors, and behaviors leave traces. Your pulse carries how the team experiences you. The calendar keeps the record of 1:1s held, moved, or quietly dropped, and a year of retention and growth tells the rest.

The evidence rule

Intentions are invisible; behavior is measurable. The standard tracks what you demonstrably do: the pulse your team answers, the 1:1s that happen or slip, and the signals your team sends with their feet and their growth.

In the room

Every manager on this call is already generating all three streams: a pulse the team just answered, a calendar that remembers which conversations happened, and a slower story told by who stays and who grows. Nothing new to install. The work is learning to read what is already there.

Talk it through: Which stream would surprise you most if you looked today, and what has kept it the one you check least?

Quick check: which of these counts as evidence of the manager standard?

Grounded in: Gallup Q12 employee engagement research

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: which of the three streams do you check least, and what might it be saying right now? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Name the evidence stream you check least, and write one guess at what it says right now.

Go deeper

Reading the three streams together

Pulse questions look soft until you know their pedigree. Gallup's Q12 research has tracked short team-experience questions against hard outcomes for decades, and the manager moves those answers more than anything else does. Even so, one stream can mislead you for a quarter: a pulse dips in a reorg, cadence slips in a crunch. The honest read is triangulation. When two streams agree for two cycles running, treat it as signal.

On your own

  1. In a private note, capture all three streams: your last pulse result, your 1:1 record for the past two months, and any team moves over the past year.
  2. Mark where two streams agree. That agreement is your strongest signal, and the behavior it points at is your best candidate for this month.
  3. Put a recurring 15-minute hold on your calendar to reread all three monthly; the read only works as a habit.

Section 02 · The Read

Ask what would move the number.

A pulse dip is a data point about how the team experienced the quarter. Tasha Eurich's self-awareness research supplies the move: swap why for what. Why is my score low invites a defense brief; what behavior would move it points somewhere you can act.

Play · Call the read

Why this matters: Three managers meet their own evidence. Call each reaction: an evidence read, a defensive read, or an overcorrection.

Grounded in: Insight, Tasha Eurich's self-awareness research (Crown)

Breakout · 3 min: Pairs: name one piece of manager feedback you argued with at first. What was the evidence read? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Recall one piece of feedback you argued with at first. Write the evidence read you could have made instead.

Go deeper

The second draft rule

Everyone writes the defensive story first when their own numbers sting; treat it as a draft, never a character flaw. The discipline is refusing to act on it. Sit with stinging data for a day, then write the evidence read in one sentence: the behavior this points at is X. If context genuinely explains the number, write the context down too, and still pick a behavior. Context and behavior can both be true.

On your own

  1. Find one number or comment from the past year that still annoys you. Write the defensive story you told first, word for word.
  2. Under it, write the one-sentence evidence read: what behavior would move this?
  3. Note which draft you actually acted on at the time, and what the second draft would have changed.

Section 03 · The Move

Pick one behavior. Make it easy to see.

The standard rewards behavior a team can actually observe. So pick the one behavior your evidence points at and stop keeping it private. Tell the team. Put it on the calendar, and ask them to flag the week it slips.

Practice · Make it visible

Why this matters: Your evidence points at one behavior. Choose what you commit to and how the team will see it.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

Designing the witness

Visibility fails quietly when nobody is assigned to notice. Borrow a trick from good goal design: name the witness, not just the behavior. Tell one specific person what to watch for and when to flag a slip; a deputized witness turns your busiest week from a loophole into a checkpoint. Then park the behavior where evidence already collects, inside the 1:1 or on the shared calendar, so proof accumulates without extra work.

On your own

  1. Write your one behavior as a sentence with a time and a place: I will do X at every Y.
  2. Pick the witness: the team member or peer who will see it most often, and tell them this week what to watch for.
  3. Check that your calendar could prove it later: recurring holds, agenda lines, anything that leaves a trace.

Your Own Evidence

Evidence of the manager standard: what gets measured.

The rule to remember

Read your own evidence monthly and make one leader behavior visible to your team.

What each part taught

2 · Ask what would move the number.

3 · Pick one behavior. Make it easy to see.

A behavior the standard can find. Your team can name it, your calendar can prove it, and May can measure it.

The recap, with the answers

1 · How is the Vanderbilt manager standard measured?

Answer: Through demonstrated behavior: pulse, cadence, team signals

The standard tracks what managers demonstrably do and what teams experience: pulse data, held 1:1s, and the signals a team sends over time.

2 · Your pulse score dips. The evidence read asks...

Answer: What behavior would move this number?

Evidence is information about behavior. The productive question aims at one behavior change, without the defensive story or the overnight overcorrection.

3 · What makes a chosen behavior count toward the standard?

Answer: It is visible: named, scheduled, observable

The standard reads what can be seen. Naming the behavior, putting it on a cadence, and letting the team observe it turns intent into evidence.

Your commitment

Choose the behavior your evidence points at. Nothing you type is saved or sent; keep any team references to roles, never names.

  • The held 1:1. Protect every 1:1 on the calendar this month; if one must move, it gets rebooked in the same week, never dropped.
  • The feedback opener. Open each 1:1 with ten minutes of real feedback and growth talk before any status updates.
  • The named commitment. Tell the team the one behavior you are working on and ask them to call it when it slips.
