05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If A manager names a real team member while sharing an aside: Protect them warmly and immediately: 'Hold that. Roles, never names.' Coaching moments belong to the person; a group call is never the place to spend one.
If A manager insists telling is faster and coaching is a luxury: Agree, once. Telling wins the moment; coaching wins the quarter. Do the math aloud: ten minutes of coaching against the tenth repeat of the same stuck.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

What if I ask a good question and get silence?

Let it sit for five full seconds; silence usually means thinking, and filling it takes the thinking back. If it truly stalls, shrink the question: 'what is one thing you have tried?' A smaller true answer beats a bigger rescued one.

Isn't GROW just another corporate acronym?

Treat it as an order, never a script: what they want, what is true, what they could try, what they will do. You can run all four beats without ever saying the word GROW. The acronym is a bookmark; the order is the tool.

What if the aside is really a performance problem?

Coaching and performance management are different conversations, and both are yours. If the gap is willingness or repeated missed standards, name it directly through the performance process. Coach the growth asides; never use coaching questions to hint at a problem you owe them plainly.

Section 01 · The Moment

Status updates hide coaching moments.

A coaching moment is any point where the subject stops being the task and becomes the person: their judgment, their confidence, their stuck. It arrives mid-update, sounds like a small aside, and is gone in ten seconds if you keep driving the task.

The tell

Listen for the sentence about themselves inside the sentences about the task: 'I never know when to escalate.' 'I hate presenting these.' The update carries the work; the aside carries the person. The aside is the door.

In the room

Picture this morning's 1:1s across Vanderbilt. A coordinator finishes an update, then adds: 'Honestly, I second-guess every one of these decisions.' In most rooms that aside gets quick reassurance or a fix, and the door closes politely. The update carried the work; the aside carried the person.

Talk it through: Think of the last aside you drove past in a 1:1. What made it easy to miss, and what was the person really telling you?

A coordinator finishes an update, then adds: 'Honestly, I second-guess every one of these decisions.' What just happened?

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs, 90 seconds each: recall an aside from a recent 1:1 you drove past. What was the person really telling you?

On your own: Recall an aside from a recent 1:1 you drove past. What was the person really telling you?

Go deeper

Steam or door: telling asides apart

Asides are trial balloons. Saying 'I second-guess every call' straight out feels risky, so people float it sideways and watch what you do with it. Drive past and the test quietly fails; the asides stop coming. One distinction sharpens your ear: steam repeats a complaint about circumstances, a door says something about the person. Steam wants a nod. A door wants one question, and one is usually all you get.

On your own

  1. List your 1:1s from the past two weeks and write down every aside about the person you can remember. Roles, never names.
  2. Mark each one steam or door: circumstances, or something about them? Steam gets a nod; a door gets a question.
  3. Pick one door and write the exact sentence you will use to reopen it at your next 1:1.

Section 02 · The Question

One question beats three instructions.

Telling is fast, and it works once. Asking is slower, and it works from then on, because the thinking happens in their head instead of yours. The craft is a question that is genuinely open: one you do not already know the answer to.

Play · Tell or ask?

Why this matters: A team member brings you a stuck. Read what the manager says back and call it.

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: take an instruction you gave this week and rewrite it together as one open question. Trade and improve.

On your own: Take an instruction you gave this week and rewrite it as one open question.

Go deeper

The second question

The first question opens the thinking; the second decides who keeps it. Whatever they answer, go one layer down before you evaluate anything: 'what makes that the hard part?' or 'what happened when you tried it?' First answers are usually the safe ones; the real material sits underneath. And when their plan is worse than yours, name the gap you see and ask what they would do about it. Graded answers dry up fast.

On your own

  1. Put three stems where you run 1:1s: 'What have you tried?' 'What is the hard part?' 'What would you do if I were out this week?'
  2. In your next stuck conversation, ask one stem, then follow the answer one layer down before you offer anything of your own.
Facilitator scriptFull 10 min · Core 7

Replace the instruction reflex with one genuinely open question that transfers the thinking.

Say

“So the door is open, and the reflex now is to be useful: three instructions, problem handled, next agenda item. Telling works, once. The reason we ask instead is where the thinking happens. Your instruction runs in your head. A real question runs in theirs, and what runs in their head is what they own. One honest question you cannot answer for them beats three instructions you can.”

Do
  1. Run Tell or ask? live; everyone plays on their own screen.
  2. After round three, pause on the fake question; ask the room where they have heard one this month.
  3. Run the breakout: rewrite one real instruction as one open question.
Ask

“What makes the fake question in round three worse than plain telling?”

Expect:

  • It pretends to hand over the thinking while keeping it
  • It adds judgment on top of the instruction
  • People learn to hear every question as a trap

Respond: All three. Plain telling is at least honest about what it is. A fake question spends trust, and trust is what your real questions will need.

Debrief

If you already know the answer you want, it is telling. Say it honestly, or ask something you genuinely cannot answer for them.

Watch for: Managers hunting for the perfect question. Lower the bar: 'what have you tried?' and 'what would you do if I were out this week?' cover most moments.

Section 03 · The Shape

Four beats keep the talk moving.

A coaching talk without a shape wanders. Here is a simple one: where are they trying to get (goal), what is actually happening (reality), what could they try (options), what will they do (will). Plain words, their answers, ten minutes.

Practice · Coach the moment

Why this matters: An advisor on your team says: 'I want the senior cases, and I keep getting passed over.' Coach it in two moves.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

Where the four beats stall

Each beat has a classic stall. Goal: taking the first answer at face value; ask what reaching it would actually get them. Reality: rushing it, which is where most bad options are born. Options: stopping at one; push for three before choosing, since even a weak third loosens the thinking. Will: a commitment without a date, which is a wish. The Canaday course and the GROW practicum are live reps for whichever beat stalls on you.

On your own

  1. Pick a real stuck on your team and write one question per beat before the next 1:1. Four lines, plain words.
  2. Run the talk, then note which beat stalled. That beat is your rep to practice next time.
  3. Start the Canaday course on LinkedIn Learning or book a navigator-led GROW practicum slot this week.

Watch for: Managers running the four beats as an interrogation checklist. It is a shape for listening; two beats done well beat four beats done fast.
