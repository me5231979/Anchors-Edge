Catch the coaching moment.

Coaching moments arrive mid-1:1, as an aside about the person inside a task update. This session teaches you to catch that aside and coach it instead of solving it.

By the end of this session, you will be able to

  1. Recognize a coaching moment inside an ordinary status conversation
  2. Trade instructions for one good question that puts the thinking in their head
  3. Run a ten-minute coaching talk with a simple four-beat shape

Section 01 · The Moment

Status updates hide coaching moments.

A coaching moment is the point where the subject stops being the task and becomes the person. It usually arrives mid-update as a small aside, and if you keep driving the task it is gone in ten seconds.

The tell

Listen for the sentence about themselves inside the sentences about the task: 'I never know when to escalate.' 'I hate presenting these.' The update carries the work; the aside carries the person. The aside is the door.

A coordinator finishes an update, then adds: 'Honestly, I second-guess every one of these decisions.' What just happened?

On your own: Recall an aside from a recent 1:1 you drove past. What was the person really telling you?

Go deeper

Steam or door: telling asides apart

Asides are trial balloons. Saying 'I second-guess every call' straight out feels risky, so people float it sideways and watch what you do with it. Drive past and the test quietly fails; the asides stop coming. One distinction sharpens your ear: steam repeats a complaint about circumstances, a door says something about the person. Steam wants a nod. A door wants one question, and one is usually all you get.

On your own

  1. List your 1:1s from the past two weeks and write down every aside about the person you can remember. Roles, never names.
  2. Mark each one steam or door: circumstances, or something about them? Steam gets a nod; a door gets a question.
  3. Pick one door and write the exact sentence you will use to reopen it at your next 1:1.

Section 02 · The Question

One question beats three instructions.

Telling is fast and works once. A question works from then on, because the thinking happens in their head. Ibarra and Scoular call this the heart of coaching: drawing insight out instead of handing answers down.

Play · Tell or ask?

Why this matters: A team member brings you a stuck. Read what the manager says back and call it.

Grounded in: The Leader as Coach, Ibarra and Scoular (HBR, 2019)

On your own: Take an instruction you gave this week and rewrite it as one open question.

Go deeper

The second question

The first question opens the thinking; the second decides who keeps it. Whatever they answer, go one layer down before you evaluate anything: 'what makes that the hard part?' or 'what happened when you tried it?' First answers are usually the safe ones; the real material sits underneath. And when their plan is worse than yours, name the gap you see and ask what they would do about it. Graded answers dry up fast.

On your own

  1. Put three stems where you run 1:1s: 'What have you tried?' 'What is the hard part?' 'What would you do if I were out this week?'
  2. In your next stuck conversation, ask one stem, then follow the answer one layer down before you offer anything of your own.

Section 03 · The Shape

Four beats keep the talk moving.

A coaching talk without a shape wanders. Sir John Whitmore's GROW model gives it one: where are they trying to get (goal), what is happening (reality), what could they try (options), what will they do (will). Plain words, their answers, ten minutes.

Practice · Coach the moment

Why this matters: An advisor on your team says: 'I want the senior cases, and I keep getting passed over.' Coach it in two moves.

Choose both steps

Grounded in: The GROW model, Sir John Whitmore (Performance Consultants)

Go deeper

Where the four beats stall

Each beat has a classic stall. Goal: taking the first answer at face value; ask what reaching it would actually get them. Reality: rushing it, which is where most bad options are born. Options: stopping at one; push for three before choosing, since even a weak third loosens the thinking. Will: a commitment without a date, which is a wish. The Canaday course and the GROW practicum are live reps for whichever beat stalls on you.

On your own

  1. Pick a real stuck on your team and write one question per beat before the next 1:1. Four lines, plain words.
  2. Run the talk, then note which beat stalled. That beat is your rep to practice next time.
  3. Start the Canaday course on LinkedIn Learning or book a navigator-led GROW practicum slot this week.
