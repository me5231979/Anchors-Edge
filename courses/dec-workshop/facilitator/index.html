05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If The projector or room screen fails: The clinic survives on paper: the walk sheet, the drafts, and the rehearsals need no screen. Read the interaction rounds aloud from your own device.
If Odd numbers leave someone without a rehearsal partner: Make one trio: two listeners catch more than one, and the third voice keeps the feedback honest.
If A manager gets visibly discouraged by a sheet full of blanks: Sit with them at the break: a sheet full of blanks usually means honest grading, which is the rarest skill in the room. One commitment is today's whole job; the rest of the sheet is a map, never a verdict.
If Someone asks whether the walk sheets get collected or reported: Answer before the worry spreads: the sheet is theirs, nothing is collected, and Goal 2 measurement runs through the formal process their HR partner owns. Today is private practice with witnesses they choose.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Does anything from this clinic feed my performance review?

No. The walk sheet stays in your hands, nothing is collected, and the room has no reporting behind it. Goal 2 measurement runs through the formal process your HR partner owns. What changes your review is the behavior itself, visible to your team over months.

My biggest gap is a behavior I honestly think does not fit my team's work. What now?

Say that out loud to your own leader rather than quietly exempting yourself; the standard expects that conversation. Draft the commitment for your second-biggest gap today so the clinic still pays, and bring the fit question to your navigator or HR partner this week.

Announcing a commitment to my team feels like admitting I have been failing them.

Teams already know your gaps; they live with them. What they rarely see is a manager naming one out loud with a date attached, and that move builds more credibility than the gap ever cost. It also models exactly what the standard asks of everyone.

Section 01 · Step One

Every line gets a date or a blank.

Gallup traces about 70 percent of a team's engagement to its manager, so this audit has earned its hour. Date each line's last instance, witness or artifact attached, or leave it blank.

What counts as evidence

Evidence is an instance: a date, plus a witness or an artifact. The 1:1 on the calendar counts, and so does written feedback logged in Oracle. A warm impression of yourself is no instance. Dates or blanks.

In the room

Monday introduced the witness test as an idea. The clinic runs it as an audit, and paper changes the experience: your year in your own handwriting, with blanks you cannot scroll past. Most managers find two surprises, evidence they forgot and a blank they were sure was covered.

Talk it through: Which blank surprised you most, and what was filling that space in your self-image before today?

Quick check: which of these counts as evidence on your walk sheet?

Grounded in: Gallup: managers drive 70 percent of engagement variance

Breakout · 3 min: At your table: name one line where your evidence surprised you. Blanks are findings, never confessions.

Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Every manager audits the full standard on paper: a date and a witness per line, or an honest blank.

Say

“Sheets out, pens up. One number before you start. Gallup has studied millions of workers and puts about 70 percent of the difference in team engagement down to a single variable, the manager. That is why this hour goes to paper and honesty. For every line of the standard, write the last instance a colleague could point to: the date, plus the witness or the artifact, like the calendar entry or the written feedback in Oracle. If nothing comes, leave it blank and move on. Nobody collects this sheet. The blanks are for you, and they are the most valuable thing you will write today.”

Do
  1. Distribute the walk sheets; the flip chart carries the evidence rule: an instance is a date plus a witness or an artifact.
  2. Give the room long quiet time; walk the tables and gently challenge suspiciously full sheets by asking for the date on one entry.
  3. Show the In the room card and run the discussion; take two or three voices on the surprising blank.
  4. Run the table exercise: one surprise per manager, stronger or weaker than expected.
Ask

“What did your sheet show you that your self-image had been covering?”

Expect:

  • A behavior I do constantly and never noticed counts
  • A blank where I was sure I was strong
  • Evidence all clustered in one good month

Respond: The clustering answer is gold: a behavior that happened once in spring is a memory, and a rhythm is what teams actually experience. Dates expose both.

Debrief

The audit's output is honest: real evidence you can stand on, and blanks that tell you where the commitment goes. Both are wins today.

Watch for: Managers grading on intentions or spiraling on blanks. Reset both the same way: blanks are findings, every sheet has them, and the next step is what blanks are for.

Transition

“Pick your most important blank. The next step turns it into something a colleague could watch.”

30-min core: Core: shorten the quiet time; managers walk the lines they already suspect and mark the rest for their month-end rewalk.

Section 02 · Step Two

Draft the commitment in three parts.

Pick your most important blank. The draft that closes it has three parts: the act, named plainly; the rhythm it repeats on; and the first instance, with a date. Anything less is an intention, and your walk sheet just showed you what intentions leave behind.

Practice · Draft the commitment

Why this matters: Take one gap from your sheet and choose how you word the commitment and how you anchor it. Wording and anchoring decide whether the gap is still blank next month.

Choose both steps

Breakout · 3 min: Table check: read your draft to the manager beside you. They test all three parts: is the act named, is there a rhythm, is the first instance dated? Fix what fails.

Facilitator scriptFull 11 min · Core 8

Turn each manager's chosen blank into a three-part commitment: act, rhythm, dated first instance, anchored outside their head.

Say

“Now the blank earns its keep. A commitment that can close a gap has three parts, and you need all three: the act, named so plainly a new hire could check it; the rhythm it repeats on, monthly or per milestone; and the first instance, with a date inside two weeks. Watch what happens without them: I will get better at feedback has none of the three, and it is exactly the sentence that produced your blank in the first place.”

Do
  1. Run Draft the commitment from the front; managers make both choices, then draft their real commitment on the walk sheet.
  2. Walk the room reading drafts; run the three-part test out loud at tables that finish early.
  3. Run the table check: drafts read aloud to a neighbor, three-part test applied, failures fixed on the spot.
Ask

“Which of the three parts was hardest to pin down?”

Expect:

  • The rhythm: how often is honest
  • The date: two weeks feels fast
  • The act: naming it small enough

Respond: Small enough is the craft. A commitment sized to impress fails by Friday; one sized to repeat becomes the habit the standard was pointing at all along.

Debrief

Act, rhythm, dated first instance, plus an anchor outside your own head. That is a commitment the standard can count.

Watch for: Drafts that commit to everything at once. One gap, one commitment; the month-end rewalk picks the next.

Transition

“The draft reads well on paper. The last step finds out if it survives your own voice.”

30-min core: Core: skip the front-of-room builder; the three-part test runs directly on real drafts at tables.

Section 03 · Step Three

Say it until it sounds like you.

A commitment lands only if the words survive your own mouth. ATD's training transfer research puts the weight on modeling and rehearsal, since most of what gets taught never reaches the job. Train the ear on three managers, then rehearse in pairs.

Play · Hear the commitment

Why this matters: Listen to each manager announce a change and label what the team can actually watch for. Tuning your ear here is what makes the pair rehearsal useful.

Grounded in: Techniques that make training transfer to the job (ATD)

Breakout · 3 min: Pair rehearsal: say your commitment as you would at the team meeting. The partner listens for anything unwitnessable, then for whether it sounds like you. Two rounds.

Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Rehearse the commitment aloud in pairs until it is witnessable language that sounds like the manager who will say it.

Say

“Words behave differently out loud. A draft that reads fine on paper can come out of your mouth as a hedge, and your team will hear the hedge. So we rehearse. ATD's research on training transfer is blunt about this, and puts the weight on watching a model and practicing out loud, because that is what survives contact with the job. First we train the ear on three managers announcing commitments; then pairs: say yours as you will say it at your team meeting. Your partner listens twice, once for anything the team could never witness, once for whether it sounds like you or like a worksheet.”

Do
  1. Run Hear the commitment from the front; the room calls each announcement before you reveal.
  2. Run the pair rehearsal, two rounds each; walk the room and listen for hedges like try and hopefully.
  3. Ask for two volunteers to say their commitment to the whole room; applaud plain language over polish.
Ask

“What did your partner catch that you did not?”

Expect:

  • A hedge word softening the whole thing
  • A state claim hiding in the middle
  • That it did not sound like me yet

Respond: The not-me answer matters most: teams smell borrowed language instantly. Keep the three parts and swap every other word for your own until it lands naturally.

Debrief

Rehearsed twice with a listener beats polished ten times alone. The words are ready when the act, rhythm, and date survive your own voice.

Watch for: Pairs drifting into discussing the gap instead of rehearsing the words. Redirect: this step is mouth practice; the analysis already happened.

Transition

“Cards next: the commitment and its first witness, on paper before you stand up.”

30-min core: Core: one rehearsal round each instead of two; skip the whole-room volunteers.
