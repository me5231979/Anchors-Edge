Curiosity is a career skill.

Careers rarely stall from lack of talent. They stall from unasked questions: about your own path, about the work happening two desks away, about what could come next. This session makes curiosity a practiced skill instead of a personality trait.

By the end of this session, you will be able to

  1. Trade vague career wondering for questions specific enough to answer this week
  2. Open a career curiosity conversation with a colleague and keep it generous
  3. Turn one curiosity into one small, dated next step

Section 01 · Your Path

Better questions beat bigger ones.

Asked in the abstract, 'what is next for me' has no answer and produces career fog. Better questions are small and answerable: what work makes me lose track of time, which of my skills do people keep borrowing? Curiosity as a skill starts at home.

The better-question test

A better career question passes two tests: someone could answer it this week, and the answer would change what you do. 'Which of my skills do people keep borrowing?' passes both. 'What is next for me?' fails both, gracefully, forever.

Quick check: which question passes the better-question test?

On your own: Write down the task in your week that makes time move fastest, and what it says about your skills.

Section 02 · The Conversations

Ask about work you don't do.

The fastest way to see the talent around you is to ask about it. A career curiosity conversation is 15 minutes with a colleague about their work: what it takes, what it teaches, what nobody sees. It costs one question and makes two people's talent visible.

Section 03 · The Move

Curiosity earns a next step.

An interesting answer that changes nothing is trivia. Curiosity becomes career fuel when the answer turns into a step small enough to actually happen: an hour of shadowing, one doc to read, one skill to try in your own work. Build the step you would take.
