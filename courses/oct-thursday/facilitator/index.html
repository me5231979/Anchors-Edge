05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If A breakout pair drifts from curiosity questions into venting about their own roles: Redirect warmly: one better question about your path counts as the exercise. Park the venting with the offer of a different forum for it.
If Someone says asking colleagues about their work feels intrusive or fake: Offer the opener live: 'I am trying to understand how work happens outside my corner; can I ask you about yours?' Fifteen minutes, their terms. Almost nobody says no to being asked well.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Isn't this just networking with a nicer name?

Networking optimizes for contacts; curiosity conversations optimize for understanding. You ask about the work on its own terms with nothing attached. The career payoff is real, and it arrives as a side effect of paying genuine attention, which is exactly why it works.

What if my curiosity points somewhere my manager would rather I not look?

Curiosity is not a transfer request. An hour of shadowing commits you to nothing, and this month's manager sessions ask managers to make exactly this kind of exploration normal. If it grows into a real move, the Talent Marketplace and the Transfer Portal exist so that it becomes a Vanderbilt move instead of an exit.

I asked a better question and got a shallow answer. Now what?

One shallow answer is data about the moment, not the method. Follow up once with the invisible-work question, which almost always deepens things. If it stays shallow, aim the same curiosity at a different person; the skill is the asking, and it compounds.

Section 01 · Your Path

Ask questions small enough to answer.

Asked in the abstract, 'what is next for me' has no answer. Harvard research on questioning finds the useful ones are specific and answerable: what work makes me lose track of time, which of my skills do people keep borrowing?

The better-question test

A better career question passes two tests: someone could answer it this week, and the answer would change what you do. 'Which of my skills do people keep borrowing?' passes both. 'What is next for me?' fails both, gracefully, forever.

In the room

Career fog is normal: most of us can describe next week's workload in detail and next year's direction not at all. The fix is not a grander plan; it is a smaller question. The test has two parts: could someone actually answer your career question this week, and would the answer change what you do?

Talk it through: What is the last question you asked about your own career, out loud or in your head? Does it pass the two tests, and if not, how would you shrink it?

Quick check: which question passes the better-question test?

Grounded in: The Surprising Power of Questions (Brooks and John, HBR)

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: which task in your week makes time move fastest? What does that say about your skills? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Write down the task in your week that makes time move fastest, and what it says about your skills.

Go deeper

Shrinking a question until it can answer you

There is a reliable way to fix a foggy career question: shrink the time frame and name who could answer it. 'What is next for me' becomes 'what did I do last month that I want more of', which becomes 'who could give me more of it, and what would I ask them'. Each shrink trades inspiration for information. The five-year question is not wrong, just downstream: you answer it by stacking small answers, not by staring at it harder.

On your own

  1. Write your current career question, however foggy. Shrink it twice: once by time frame, once by naming a person who could answer it.
  2. Answer the smallest version this week, then note what it tells you about the bigger one.

Say

“We tend to treat curiosity as a personality trait you either have or you don't. It works better as a technique, and the technique is the question you ask. So today we practice on the question you aim at your own career, specific enough to answer this week and honest enough that the answer changes what you do.”

Ask

“Honestly, what is the last question you asked about your own career?”

Expect:

  • Am I doing okay
  • What is next for me
  • Often silence: no question at all

Respond: All normal. The silence is the finding: most of us go months without asking our career a single answerable question. Today fixes the question first, then the habit.

Debrief

A better question is answerable this week and changes what you do. Everything else is fog.

Watch for: People turning the prompt into self-criticism. Keep it at curiosity about skills, never a verdict on progress.

Section 02 · The Conversations

Ask about work you don't do.

The fastest way to see the talent around you is to ask about it. A career curiosity conversation is 15 minutes with a colleague about their work, what it actually takes and what nobody sees. One good question makes two people's talent visible.

Play · Door or dead end?

Why this matters: Read each moment and call it: real curiosity, a dead end, or judgment. A question that is specific, generous, and about the other person is what gets honest answers.

Breakout · 3 min: Pairs: whose work at Vanderbilt are you quietly curious about? Say the role and the question you would open with.

On your own: Name a role at Vanderbilt you are quietly curious about, and write the question you would open with.

Go deeper

The second question is where the door opens

First answers are usually rehearsed; everyone has a polished line about their job. The craft of a curiosity conversation is the second question, the one that follows their answer instead of your script: what surprised you about that, when did it last go wrong, what would make it easier. Follow-ups prove you were listening, and being listened to is what makes people show you the real work. The real work is where the talent is.

On your own

  1. In your next conversation about anyone's work, ask one planned question, then only follow-ups for five minutes. Notice where it goes.
  2. Afterward, write down the one thing you learned that a scripted list of questions would have missed.
Facilitator scriptFull 11 min · Core 8

Equip learners to open generous career curiosity conversations and to hear the difference between curiosity, dead ends, and judgment.

Say

“This month is called See the Talent Around You, and the mechanism is almost embarrassingly simple: talent becomes visible when somebody asks about it. A career curiosity conversation is fifteen minutes about a colleague's work on its own terms, no agenda, nothing to get. What does the work actually take? What does nobody see? Let's train your ear for what real curiosity sounds like.”

Do
  1. Run Door or dead end? live; everyone plays on their own screen.
  2. After round two, pause: ask who has been on the receiving end of a dead-end question and felt the difference.
  3. Run the breakout on the role they are quietly curious about.
Ask

“Why does 'what does nobody see about your work' open people up so reliably?”

Expect:

  • Everyone has an invisible part of their job
  • It signals respect for the real work
  • Nobody ever asks it

Respond: All three. It hands someone the microphone for the part of their work that goes unthanked. You learn the truth of the role, they feel seen, and both halves serve this month's theme.

Debrief

Real curiosity is specific, generous, and about them. Dead ends fish for reassurance; judgment closes the doors it knocks on.

Watch for: People framing the conversation as networking for advantage. Keep it generous; the visibility payoff is mutual and arrives on its own.

Transition

“A good answer is the beginning, though. Stop three turns one into a step.”

Section 03 · The Move

Turn the answer into a next step.

An answer starts helping your career once it shrinks to a step you can take this week. ATD's learning transfer research finds the step people write down and date is the one they take.

Practice · Book the next step

Why this matters: You just had a great career curiosity conversation. Choose what you do with the answer, and when.

Choose both steps

Grounded in: Practical learning transfer techniques (ATD)

Go deeper

Why small asks get yes

Small asks work because they price the favor honestly. An hour of shadowing costs the other person almost nothing and honors the work you are asking about; a formal rotation request costs approvals, cover, and risk. Small steps also generate their own sequel: the shadow hour surfaces one doc worth reading; the doc surfaces one meeting worth sitting in. Careers move on chains of small yeses more often than on single big ones.

On your own

  1. Draft the one-message ask for your curiosity: one hour, one meeting, or one doc. Cut it until it fits in three sentences.
  2. Send it, and put a placeholder hold on your own calendar so the yes has somewhere to land.
Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Convert one curiosity into a small next step with a date on it.

Say

“Curiosity usually dies right after the good conversation. The answer was fascinating, everyone meant to follow up, and nothing happened. ATD's work on learning transfer puts a number on the fix: when people leave a session with a written, dated next step, they are roughly twice as likely to actually do it. Same mechanism here. Ask for the smallest real taste of the thing, write it down, and put a date on it before the feeling fades. An hour of shadowing books in one message. Let's build the step.”

Do
  1. Run Book the next step; give the room two minutes to make both choices and run it.
  2. Ask one volunteer to read their outcome aloud, weak or strong.
  3. Point at the pattern: smallest real taste, date before you log off.
Ask

“Why does 'someday, when work calms down' feel so reasonable and work so badly?”

Expect:

  • Work never calms down
  • The curiosity fades before the calendar clears

Respond: Both. Someday is a real decision disguised as a delay. The small dated step is how you outvote your own busyness.

Debrief

Small enough to get a yes, dated enough to be real. Nothing else converts an answer into a career move.

Watch for: People sizing the step too big, like requesting a formal rotation. Shrink it until the ask fits in one message.
