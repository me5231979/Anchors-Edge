05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If A manager pushes back that they cannot afford to lose anyone right now: Validate the pressure, then do the math aloud: a supported move keeps the person at Vanderbilt and earns you an ally; a blocked one usually costs the person entirely within the year.
If Someone names a real employee while describing a live internal application: Protect them warmly and immediately: 'Hold that. Roles, never names.' The rehearsal works on patterns, never on a colleague's live confidential move.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

If I encourage internal moves, won't my best people all leave at once?

Teams with sending managers show the opposite pattern: people stay longer because staying costs them no growth. The moves that do happen are staggered, planned, and keep the person one call away instead of gone from Vanderbilt.

What if I genuinely can't spare the person for months?

Timing is a fair conversation, and the Portal expects transition planning. Say what you can honestly support and work the timeline with the hiring manager. The line to hold: the move happens, and you help shape when and how, never whether.

How is this different from just losing headcount?

The position stays with your team; the person stays with Vanderbilt. You backfill a role, which is normal management, and you gain an advocate in another department. Compare that to an external resignation, where you backfill anyway and gain nothing.

Section 01 · The Mindset

An internal move is a retention win.

When someone moves from your team to another Vanderbilt role, the university keeps a proven person. Holding them in place works for a while, and then a recruiter calls. Gartner has a name for that pattern: talent hoarding.

The sending math

Hold someone past their growth and their next move is usually out the front door. Send them to another Vanderbilt role and the university keeps them. Hoarding feels like retention; it is a slow leak with your name on it.

In the room

Last month trained your eye to see the talent already around you; sending is what the seeing is for. Gartner's research puts numbers on the stakes: roughly one in three employees believes they could contribute more in a different role at their own organization. The Portal exists so they move inside.

Talk it through: Think of a time holding someone in place felt like retention. Looking back, what did it actually cost the person, your team, or Vanderbilt?

Your strongest coordinator is a finalist for a role in another school. What did Vanderbilt just do?

Grounded in: Talent hoarding research, Gartner HR (2024)

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: think of someone who left your team, for anywhere. Would a Vanderbilt move have kept them? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Think of someone who left your team. Would an internal move have kept them at Vanderbilt?

Go deeper

The hard case: the person you cannot spare

The mindset gets tested hardest with the person you truly cannot spare this quarter. The trade stays the same: a supported move on a negotiated timeline keeps them at Vanderbilt and keeps your credibility, while a blocked one starts an external search you will never see. The Portal expects transition planning, so timing is always a fair conversation. Whether is the thing you never negotiate, and people can tell the difference.

On your own

  1. List who on your team is ready for more than their role offers. Roles on paper, never names.
  2. For each, note the internal move that would keep them at Vanderbilt if it opened tomorrow.
  3. Write the one handoff risk you would need to plan for, so your timing talk stays honest.

Section 02 · The Moment

The first sixty seconds decide the story.

Someone gathers the nerve to tell you they applied internally. Whatever you say in the next minute becomes the story they tell their colleagues. Read each manager reply below and call what it really is.

Play · Call the response

Why this matters: People replay a manager's first reaction for years. Read each reply and call it.

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: what would you honestly feel in that first minute, and what do you want to say anyway? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Write the first sentence you want to say when someone tells you they applied internally.

Go deeper

Two harder cousins of the moment

The rehearsed minute has two harder cousins. First: you hear about the application from someone else. Open with the same gratitude anyway; a defensive first line confirms every reason they stayed quiet. Second: they do not get the role. The week after a miss decides whether they ever raise a hand again, so treat it as career data: debrief what the hiring team wanted and name the skill to build next.

On your own

  1. Write your opening sentence for each cousin: the day you hear it secondhand, and the week after a miss.
  2. Say both out loud once. Your mouth finds the stiff phrases faster than your eyes do.
Facilitator scriptFull 11 min · Core 8

Rehearse the first sixty seconds after someone says they applied internally, so the trained response beats the reflex.

Say

“At some point this year, someone will gather their nerve for a week and then tell you they applied for an internal role. Your face and your first sentence become the story they tell. The reflex is loss, and the reflex will show unless you have rehearsed something better. So we rehearse. Say thank you, get curious about the role, then back them where people can hear it. The Portal has no secrecy in it, and neither should you.”

Do
  1. Run Call the response live; everyone plays on their own screen.
  2. After round three, pause on the hedge: ask why 'keep it between us' undoes the support.
  3. Run the breakout on what they would honestly feel in that minute.
Ask

“What makes the hedge in round three worse than it sounds?”

Expect:

  • It makes mobility feel illicit
  • It protects the manager rather than the person
  • It contradicts the open Portal norm

Respond: All of it. Secrecy converts a supported move into a guilty one. If the move is good, it can be public; if it needs hiding, ask yourself from whom.

Debrief

Feel the loss privately and give the backing publicly. Rehearsed words are how the better manager shows up first.

Watch for: Managers arguing the hedge is reasonable during a busy season. Acknowledge the pressure, then re-anchor: timing talk is fine; secrecy is never part of it.

Section 03 · The Reputation

Send-offs are recruiting.

Every departure from your team gets watched, and by more people than the one leaving. Build the send-off that makes your team the one people ask to join.

Practice · Build the send-off

Why this matters: Someone on your team just accepted an internal move. Choose how you announce it and how you run their last two weeks.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

The alumni ledger

A sending reputation compounds through alumni. Everyone who leaves your team well becomes a standing bridge: they route collaborations back, vouch for your openings, and flag work headed your way before the org chart knows. A manager with a few well-sent alumni across the university hears things months early. The send-off is where that ledger gets its first entry, and it is cheap to write.

On your own

  1. List everyone who left your team in recent years and where they landed. Departments and roles, never names.
  2. Mark the ones you could comfortably call today. That count is your sending reputation so far.
  3. For one bridge gone cold, draft the two-minute message that reopens the door.
Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Show that public send-offs are recruiting, and build one worth copying.

Say

“Nobody reads your leadership philosophy. Everybody watches your send-offs. When someone leaves your team for another department, the person leaving, the team staying, and everyone deciding where to grow next are all taking notes. A manager people grow and go well from never has an empty pipeline. So let's build the send-off that earns that.”

Do
  1. Run Build the send-off; two minutes to choose both slots and run it.
  2. Ask one volunteer to read their outcome aloud, weak or strong.
  3. Point at the pattern: public pride plus a protected relationship.
Ask

“Why does the quiet exit hurt more than it seems?”

Expect:

  • The team invents the story
  • It signals moves are betrayals
  • The mover retells it elsewhere

Respond: Right. Silence is never neutral; it is the worst story, told by someone else. The announcement is the cheapest reputation move you have.

Debrief

A send-off has three watchers: the mover, the team, and the people deciding where to grow next. Build it for all three.

Watch for: Managers stuck on backfill anxiety. It is real and worth its own conversation with HR; keep it from swallowing the reputation point.
