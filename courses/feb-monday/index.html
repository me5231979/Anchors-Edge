Manager Monday · The manager lens · Retain, Grow, Move

Succession is a growth plan.

  2. Open a succession conversation without triggering replacement fear

Begin the session

Section 01 · The Reframe

Succession planning is growth planning.

A succession plan is a list of people getting ready for bigger work, and what they need next. HBR's review of the research backs this reading: succession pays off when it runs as ongoing development, while the scramble-for-a-replacement version costs dearly.

The reframe

A succession plan answers one question: who is getting ready for bigger work, and what do they need next? Read that way, the bench stops being about exits and becomes a concrete growth plan for the people on it.

In the room

Succession plans often exist without the people on them knowing, because the word feels risky to say. The document is identical either way. The framing decides whether the plan retains someone or quietly worries them, and the framing is the manager's choice.

Talk it through: What makes managers keep succession plans secret from the very people on them? Name the fear honestly, then name what the secrecy costs.

Quick check: what does being named to a bench actually mean for the person on it?

Grounded in: Succession Planning: What the Research Says, Eben Harrell (Harvard Business Review)

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: say the sentence 'you are on the bench for X' out loud. Does it land as a gift or a threat? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Write the sentence 'you are on the bench for X' for one person on your team. Does it read as a gift or a threat?

Go deeper

When the seat is declined

Not everyone wants bigger work, and a healthy bench survives a no. The offer still pays: the person learns they are seen, you learn their real direction, and the conversation often surfaces a growth path sideways instead of up. A declined seat only damages trust when it was framed as an expectation. Keep it an invitation with something attached now, whatever they decide about later.

On your own

  1. Pick one person who might say no to a bench seat and write the invitation version: the growth you see, with zero pressure attached.
  2. List two things the seat pays this quarter, stretch work or visibility, that stand even if the bigger role never interests them.
  3. Draft your reply to a flat no in two sentences, so the conversation ends with the trust intact.

Section 02 · The Conversation

Open it so they hear investment.

How you open decides how the news lands. An opening built on the org chart triggers replacement fear before you finish talking. Lead with the person's growth and what starts now, and you earn the whole conversation.

Play · Gift or threat?

Why this matters: Read each opening line and sort how the listener hears it: investment, replacement fear, or too vague to act on. Watch what the first sentence leads with.

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: each of you says your opening line for a succession conversation. Partner answers with the first thing they would feel.

On your own: Write your opening line for a succession conversation, then reread it as the listener. What is the first thing you would feel?

Go deeper

The second sentence matters too

A strong opening buys you about thirty seconds of trust, and the second sentence decides what you do with it. The trap is over-promising: 'ready for bigger work' slides into 'the next opening is yours' under the warmth of the moment, and a promise you cannot keep costs more than the fear you avoided. Honest succession language has three ingredients: the growth you see, what starts now, and what you cannot promise.

On your own

  1. Write your full opening: sentence one names the growth you see, sentence two names what starts now, sentence three names what you cannot promise.
  2. Read it aloud and cut anything that sounds like a guarantee. Readiness and direction survive; timelines and titles go.

Section 03 · The Move

Your role needs a pipeline too.

Build a pipeline for your own role as well. A manager with a bench is freed for bigger work and models the practice for the team. Two decisions start it: who goes on, and what you say to them.

Practice · Start your pipeline

Why this matters: Two decisions start a pipeline for your own role. Choose how you pick and how you say it.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

Skills first, on paper

Most managers can list their tasks and freeze on their skills. Run the deputy test: if you were out for a month, which decisions would wobble? Those decisions are the real skills of your role. Then map people against that list looking for partial overlap plus appetite. Nobody should tick every box; a bench candidate needs a visible growth gap, because the gap is what you grow. A finished copy of you is a hire, not a bench.

On your own

  1. Run the deputy test: list the five decisions that would wobble if you were out for a month. Those are your role's real skills.
  2. Map two or three people against that list, looking for partial overlap plus appetite, never a finished copy of you.
  3. Pick the first stretch handoff for one of them and put a date on the conversation.

The core idea

Tell people they are on the bench, and name the growth that starts now.
