05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If A manager shares a story with private details about a named employee's AI mistake: Protect them warmly and immediately: 'Hold that. Roles, never names.' The lesson travels fine without the person attached.
If The room splits between AI enthusiasts and skeptics: Give each side one sentence, then point at the traffic light: it gives enthusiasts a lane and skeptics a boundary, and it asks both for the same small move.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

I have never used an AI tool myself. How can I set the tone?

Start where you are; that is the month's theme. Pick one green-light chore, try it this week, and model it honestly, fixes and all. A manager learning out loud sets a better tone than an expert performing. The navigator-led First-AI-Win sandbox session is a guided place to do that first run.

What if someone on my team is already pasting internal content into free tools?

Treat it as a signal of appetite, and address the boundary without shaming the experiment. Teach the traffic light, route the yellow work into approved VU tools, and thank them for surfacing it. Curiosity is fuel you want; it just needs the guardrails.

Is AI adoption really my job? We have IT and a policy for that.

Policy and platforms set the outer boundary; daily behavior forms around what you permit and model. Your team builds its habits around you long before anyone reads a policy page. That is why this month treats adoption as a leadership responsibility, and why the ask is one modeled chore, small enough for any calendar.

Section 01 · The Tone

Silence is also a signal.

Ask a team why they avoid AI and you usually hear that the manager never brought it up. Your tone gets read whether you set it or drift into it. This month you set it on purpose, and you start by saying out loud what is allowed.

The tone rule

A team's AI behavior mirrors its manager's signals. They listen for three things: permission (may we?), safety (what is in bounds?), and curiosity (does trying count?). When all three are clear, safe experiments start.

In the room

McKinsey's Superagency research found employees readier for AI than their leaders assume; the pace of adoption is set higher up. Your team is probably not waiting on training or news coverage. They are waiting to hear from you what is allowed and what is smart.

Talk it through: Where did your own read on AI come from: a manager, a policy page, or the news? What does that say about what your team is reading from you right now?

Quick check: a manager has never mentioned AI to the team. What does the team most likely conclude?

Grounded in: Superagency in the workplace, McKinsey (2025)

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: what tone has your team read from you about AI so far, on purpose or by accident? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Write one line: what tone has your team read from you about AI so far, on purpose or by accident?

Go deeper

The three signals, decoded

Each signal has a telltale question behind it. Permission: has my manager ever said out loud that we may use AI here? Safety: could I state the boundary in one sentence if a new hire asked? Curiosity: when someone mentions an experiment, does the manager lean in or change the subject? Teams answer these from small moments, like what you laughed at, what you ignored, and what you asked about. A tone audit starts there.

On your own

  1. Answer the three questions the way your team would, based only on what they have watched you do and say.
  2. Pick the weakest signal and script one sentence that makes it explicit at your next team meeting.

Section 02 · The Model

Model the first green light.

The shared traffic light keeps this simple. Green, public or generic information: go. Yellow, internal work: approved VU tools only. Red, private information about people: never. Pick a boring green chore and let your team watch you run it, fixes and all.

Play · Green, yellow, or red?

Why this matters: Your team will bring you cases exactly like these. Call each one before you have to answer theirs.

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: name one green-light chore from your own week you could model for your team this month. 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Name one green-light chore from your own week you could model for your team this month.

Go deeper

When the light is hard to call

Real chores mix information, and the most sensitive piece decides the light for the whole task. A job posting built from the public description is green; paste in notes from the finalist interviews and the same task turns red. That gives you a second move to model: strip the chore. Remove the internal or personal specifics, run the generic skeleton on green, and add the sensitive parts back yourself afterward.

On your own

  1. List two chores from your week that feel yellow or red, and find the green skeleton inside each by removing specifics.
  2. Write the exact input you would give the tool for one of them, then reread it for anything internal or personal.

Expect:

  • Boring is easy to copy
  • Impressive demos create distance
  • Low stakes means mistakes teach cheaply

Respond: Right. The goal is a team full of small safe experiments, and those start from a chore everyone recognizes.

Debrief

The information decides the light every time. Green goes, yellow stays inside approved VU tools, red never goes in.

Watch for: Managers inventing edge cases to postpone action. Park edge cases in chat for the tough-questions bank and bring the room back to their one green chore.

Section 03 · The Talk

Talk about it like a leader.

Hype earns eye rolls; fear drives experiments underground. The conversation that works is plain: here is what I tried, here is what is in bounds, and I want to hear what you try. Build the version you would actually say at your next team meeting.

Practice · Open the AI conversation

Why this matters: Your team meeting, five minutes on the agenda. Choose how you open and what you invite.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

Keeping the conversation alive

One good conversation sets the tone; a small ritual keeps it. Reserve two minutes of each team meeting for one AI share, with the same beats you modeled: what I tried, what it did well, what I fixed. Expect silence for the first week or two, and fill it with another small use of your own rather than pressing anyone. Once results get shared out loud, checking becomes a habit the team keeps visible for itself.

On your own

  1. Add a standing two-minute item called one AI share to your team agenda for the next month.
  2. Prepare two of your own shares in advance so early silence never stalls the ritual.
  3. When the first volunteer shares, ask what they fixed; that question keeps the editor habit in view.

Say

“Last piece: the words. Oversell this and people tune out. Scare them and the experiments go underground, which is where the real risk lives. What works is plain talk: what I tried, what is in bounds, what I want to hear back. Let's build yours.”

Do

Ask

“What makes 'be sensible about what you put in' a weak guardrail?”

Expect:

  • Everyone defines sensible differently
  • It pushes the judgment onto the least informed person

Respond: Exactly. The traffic light exists so nobody has to improvise the boundary. Give the rule, then invite the experiment.

Debrief

The strong conversation has a shape: I tried this, here is the light system, bring me what you try.

Watch for: Managers drafting a policy memo in their heads. Keep it spoken and small; the tone lives in the meeting, and the memo can follow.
