Section 01 · The Tone

Silence is also a signal.

Ask a team why they avoid AI and you usually hear that the manager never brought it up. People read your tone whether you set it or drift into it, so this month you set it on purpose: clear permission, visible guardrails, and honest curiosity.

The tone rule

A team's AI behavior mirrors its manager's signals. They listen for three things: permission (may we?), safety (what is in bounds?), and curiosity (does trying count?). When all three are clear, safe experiments start.

Quick check: a manager has never mentioned AI to the team. What does the team most likely conclude?

On your own: Write one line: what tone has your team read from you about AI so far, on purpose or by accident?

Go deeper

The three signals, decoded

Each signal has a telltale question behind it. Permission: has my manager ever said out loud that we may use AI here? Safety: could I state the boundary in one sentence if a new hire asked? Curiosity: when someone mentions an experiment, does the manager lean in or change the subject? Teams answer these from small moments, like what you laughed at, what you ignored, and what you asked about. A tone audit starts there.

On your own

  1. Answer the three questions the way your team would, based only on what they have watched you do and say.
  2. Pick the weakest signal and script one sentence that makes it explicit at your next team meeting.

Section 02 · The Model

Model the first green light.

The shared traffic light keeps this simple. Green, public or generic information: go. Yellow, internal work: approved VU tools only. Red, private information about people: never. Pick a boring green chore and let your team watch you run it, fixes and all.

Play · Green, yellow, or red?

Why this matters: Your team will bring you cases exactly like these. Call each one before you have to answer theirs.

On your own: Name one green-light chore from your own week you could model for your team this month.

Go deeper

When the light is hard to call

Real chores mix information, and the most sensitive piece decides the light for the whole task. A job posting built from the public description is green; paste in notes from the finalist interviews and the same task turns red. That gives you a second move to model: strip the chore. Remove the internal or personal specifics, run the generic skeleton on green, and add the sensitive parts back yourself afterward.

On your own

  1. List two chores from your week that feel yellow or red, and find the green skeleton inside each by removing specifics.
  2. Write the exact input you would give the tool for one of them, then reread it for anything internal or personal.

Section 03 · The Talk

Talk about it like a leader.

Hype earns eye rolls; fear drives experiments underground. The conversation that works is plain: here is what I tried, here is what is in bounds, and I want to hear what you try. Build the version you would actually say at your next team meeting.

Go deeper

Keeping the conversation alive

One good conversation sets the tone; a small ritual keeps it. Reserve two minutes of each team meeting for one AI share, with the same beats you modeled: what I tried, what it did well, what I fixed. Expect silence for the first week or two, and fill it with another small use of your own rather than pressing anyone. Once results get shared out loud, checking becomes a habit the team keeps visible for itself.

On your own

  1. Add a standing two-minute item called one AI share to your team agenda for the next month.
  2. Prepare two of your own shares in advance so early silence never stalls the ritual.
  3. When the first volunteer shares, ask what they fixed; that question keeps the editor habit in view.
