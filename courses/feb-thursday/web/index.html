Section 01 · The Shift

The message rides the tone.

Every conversation runs on two channels: the words, and everything around them. When a chatty teammate answers in one line, or a steady voice speeds up, the second channel is broadcasting. The skill starts with noticing the change from that person's normal.

The baseline rule

Shifts only mean something against a baseline. Quiet is no signal from a quiet person; it is a loud one from a talker. Read the change from this person's normal, and hold your first guess about what it means loosely.

A teammate who usually fills the room writes 'fine, whatever works' about a plan they built. What matters most here?

On your own: Name one 'tell' you have learned to read in a colleague: a shift in tone, pace, or energy. Roles, never names.

Section 02 · The Check-In

Ask the shift, lightly.

A noticed shift is a hypothesis. Guessing turns it into a verdict ('you're clearly upset') and ignoring lets it harden on its own. The move in between is a light, private, open check-in that lets the person tell you what is true, including 'all good'.

Play · Assume, ignore, or check in?

Why this matters: You noticed a shift. Read what happens next and call the move.

On your own: Write one check-in line you could actually use tomorrow, in your own voice.

Section 03 · The Response

Answer the feeling, then the facts.

When a message arrives carrying frustration, most of us answer the content and skip the feeling, and the sender hears half a reply. Answering the feeling takes one plain sentence, and it is the sentence that makes the rest of your answer land as help.

Practice · Answer the whole message

Why this matters: A colleague writes: 'The data STILL isn't ready. I've asked three times and my report is due Friday. Whatever, I'll figure it out.' Build your reply.
