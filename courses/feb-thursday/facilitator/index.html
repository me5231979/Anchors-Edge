05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If Someone names a real colleague during a breakout or share-out: Protect them warmly and immediately: 'Hold that. Roles, never names.' Advocacy is practiced in real rooms, never rehearsed with real names on a group call.
If A participant says nobody senior has ever seen their work: Point at the showing move: seen is arranged, never waited for. One finished piece of work, one walk-through, and the evidence problem is solved in thirty minutes.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Isn't asking someone to sponsor me just asking for favoritism?

Favoritism is backing without evidence. Sponsorship is backing built on work the sponsor has actually seen, which is why the showing comes before the ask. You are asking someone to repeat what they know firsthand, and that is how good staffing decisions get made.

I am early in my career. Do I have any credibility to spend on someone else?

You have rooms: team meetings, chats, peers asking who could help. Peer advocacy is real advocacy; a name plus evidence carries weight at every level, and giving it is how you learn to earn it.

What if I sponsor someone and their work disappoints?

That risk is exactly what makes sponsorship valuable, so manage it like a sponsor does: back work you have genuinely seen, be specific about what you are vouching for, and treat one miss as information rather than a reason to stop spending.

Section 01 · The Difference

Mentors advise. Sponsors spend.

Mentoring is advice given to you: guidance, feedback, a listening ear. Sponsorship is someone spending their credibility on you: naming you for a project, backing your move in a room you are not in. Both matter; careers stall with only the first.

Play · Advice or advocacy?

Why this matters: The two get called mentoring interchangeably, and they are wildly different currencies. Call each one.

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: think of the last real help your career got. Was it advice or advocacy? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Think of the last real help your career got. Write down whether it was advice or advocacy.

Go deeper

Why sponsorship stays invisible

Sponsorship is hard to learn by watching because it happens where you are absent, so most people conclude it barely exists and over-invest in collecting advice. There is a second confusion: one person can be both. The director who coaches your slides on Tuesday might stake their name on you in Thursday's staffing meeting. The relationship is not the currency; the act is. Track acts and you see who actually spends for whom.

On your own

  1. List the three people who have most helped your career, then mark each act of help A for advice or S for sponsorship. Notice the ratio.
  2. Write down one decision that went your way where you never learned who spoke for you. Someone probably did.
Facilitator scriptFull 8 min · Core 6

Say

“Think about the best career help you ever got. For most of us it was advice: a mentor, a coach, someone generous with guidance. Advice matters, and careers rarely move on advice alone. They move when somebody says your name in a room you are not in and stakes a little of their own reputation on it. That second thing has a name: sponsorship. People who cannot tell the two apart keep asking for advice when what they need is backing.”

Do

  2. After round two, pause on the cost: what does the director risk that the helpful analyst does not?

Ask

“Why do the kind words in round three feel so much like sponsorship?”

Expect:

  • They are flattering
  • They come from someone credible
  • They sound like a prediction about your future

Respond: Right, and nothing traveled. The test is always the same: did the words reach someone with a decision to make, and did they cost the speaker anything?

Debrief

Advice is words to you. Advocacy is risk taken for you, somewhere you are not. Both are gifts; only one moves careers on its own.

Watch for: People concluding mentors are worthless. Keep both currencies valuable: advice builds the work, advocacy moves it.

Section 02 · The Ask

Sponsorship is earned, then asked for.

Sponsors risk their reputation, so they back work they have seen and trust. That gives you two levers: make your work visible enough to stake a name on, and ask for the backing directly. Vague hoping does neither. Build both moves.

Practice · Build the ask

Why this matters: Advocacy rarely arrives unrequested. Choose how you make your work sponsorable and how you ask.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

Choosing the right sponsor

Not every senior person can sponsor you; the credibility has to be spendable in the rooms you need. Look for someone who has actually seen your finished work, sits in or near the rooms where your kind of chance gets decided, and has a record of saying names other than their own favorites. One well-placed sponsor beats three prestigious ones. And keep the ask bounded to one room and one chance, so their yes is cheap to give.

On your own

  1. Name the room where your next chance gets decided, then list who is actually in it. Your sponsor search starts on that list.
  2. Pick the one person on that list who has seen your finished work. If nobody has, your first move is the showing, not the ask.
Facilitator scriptFull 10 min · Core 7

Give everyone a concrete path to sponsorship: visible finished work plus a specific, bounded ask.

Say

“Sponsorship sounds like something that happens to lucky people. It is closer to a transaction with two halves. A sponsor spends reputation, so they need evidence: work they have seen up close. And sponsors are busy, so they need the ask said out loud, with a room attached. Show the work, name the room. People who do both get backed at rates that look like luck from the outside.”

Do
  1. Run Build the ask; two minutes for both choices, then run it.
  2. Ask one volunteer to read their outcome aloud, weak or strong.
  3. Point at the pattern: evidence a sponsor can repeat, an ask with a room attached.
Ask

“What makes the direct ask feel so hard?”

Expect:

  • It feels like begging for favors
  • Fear of hearing no
  • Not knowing whom to ask

Respond: Reframe it: a specific, bounded ask is easy to grant and easy to decline, which makes it respectful in both directions. The vague hope is the real imposition.

Debrief

Sponsorable means seen: one finished piece of work, walked through. Asked means named: this room, this chance, my name.

Watch for: People whose ask is really a promotion request. Keep the ask pointed at a specific room or chance, never a title.

Transition

Section 03 · The Give

Advocacy is also a skill you give.

You do not need a title to sponsor someone. Everyone sits in rooms a colleague is absent from: meetings, chats, hallway asks for a name. Speaking up there, with evidence, is sponsorship at any level. The test is whether you can name who you do it for.

The advocacy question

Whose name do you say in rooms they are not in? If the answer is nobody, you are holding credibility and spending none of it. Pick one colleague whose work you have seen, and say their name the next time the room asks who could do this.

In the room

Right now, somewhere at Vanderbilt, a staffing conversation is asking who could take this on. The names that get said belong to people whose work someone in the room has seen. Every person on this call is the potential sayer of a name this week, whatever their title.

Talk it through: Whose name do you actually say in rooms they are not in, roles only? If the honest answer is nobody, what would it take to change that this week?

Quick check: what turns a compliment into an act of sponsorship?

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: whose work have you actually spoken up for lately, role only? If nobody comes to mind, say that. 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Write the role of one person whose work you have spoken up for lately. If nobody comes to mind, write that down too.

Go deeper

Sponsorship with a light touch

Giving advocacy has its own craft: vouch for the work, not the whole person. 'I have seen their process mapping and it was excellent' is spendable and survives a miss; 'they are amazing' is a blank check. Match the size of your claim to what you have actually seen, name the evidence, and let the decision-maker do the rest. Small accurate claims, repeated over time, build more careers than one grand endorsement.

On your own

  1. Pick one colleague, role only, and write the one-sentence vouch: the specific work you saw and what it tells a decision-maker.
  2. Identify the next room or thread where that sentence would count, and decide now that you will say it there.

  • Some are realizing it right now, on the call

Debrief

Advocacy at any level: a name, the work you have seen, a room where it counts. If your answer to the question is nobody, pick someone this week.

Watch for: Names slipping out in the breakout. Warmly enforce roles only; even praise stays roles-never-names on a group call.
