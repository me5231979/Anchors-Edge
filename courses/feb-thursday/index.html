Begin the session

Section 01 · The Difference

Mentors advise. Sponsors spend.

Sylvia Ann Hewlett's line is simple: mentors advise you, sponsors spend their credibility on you, naming you for real work in rooms you never see. Careers stall on the first alone.

Play · Advice or advocacy?

Why this matters: Sort each situation: advice given to the person, credibility spent on them, or kind words that are neither yet. Ask what the help cost the giver.

Grounded in: The Sponsor Effect, Sylvia Ann Hewlett (HBR Press)

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: think of the last real help your career got. Was it advice or advocacy? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Think of the last real help your career got. Write down whether it was advice or advocacy.

Go deeper

Why sponsorship stays invisible

Sponsorship is hard to learn by watching because it happens where you are absent, so most people conclude it barely exists and over-invest in collecting advice. There is a second confusion: one person can be both. The director who coaches your slides on Tuesday might stake their name on you in Thursday's staffing meeting. The currency lives in the act, whoever performs it. Track acts and you see who actually spends for whom.

On your own

  1. List the three people who have most helped your career, then mark each act of help A for advice or S for sponsorship. Notice the ratio.
  2. Write down one decision that went your way where you never learned who spoke for you. Someone probably did.

Section 02 · The Ask

Sponsorship is earned, then asked for.

Sponsors back work they have seen; their reputation rides on it. Hewlett's research is blunt about what follows: people with a sponsor ask for stretch work and raises far more often, and get them far more often. Be seen, then ask.

Practice · Build the ask

Why this matters: Advocacy rarely arrives unrequested. Choose how you make your work sponsorable and how you ask.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

Choosing the right sponsor

Not every senior person can sponsor you; the credibility has to be spendable in the rooms you need. Look for someone who has actually seen your finished work, sits in or near the rooms where your kind of chance gets decided, and has a record of saying names other than their own favorites. One well-placed sponsor beats three prestigious ones. And keep the ask bounded to one room and one chance, so their yes is cheap to give.

On your own

  1. Name the room where your next chance gets decided, then list who is actually in it. Your sponsor search starts on that list.
  2. Pick the one person on that list who has seen your finished work. If nobody has, your first move is the showing, not the ask.

Section 03 · The Give

Advocacy is also a skill you give.

You do not need a title to sponsor someone. SHRM describes sponsorship as explicit advocacy, saying a person's name in rooms they are not in. You sit in those rooms weekly, so the test is whether you can name who you spend that credibility on.

The advocacy question

Whose name do you say in rooms they are not in? If the answer is nobody, you are holding credibility and spending none of it. Pick one colleague whose work you have seen, and say their name the next time the room asks who could do this.

In the room

Staffing conversations regularly come down to one question: who could take this on? The names that get said belong to people whose work someone in the room has seen. Whatever your title, you sit in rooms like that every week, and a colleague's name is yours to say there.

Talk it through: Whose name do you actually say in rooms they are not in, roles only? If the honest answer is nobody, what would it take to change that this week?

Quick check: what turns a compliment into an act of sponsorship?

Grounded in: Mentorship and Sponsorship: Core Tools for Talent Growth (SHRM)

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: whose work have you actually spoken up for lately, role only? If nobody comes to mind, say that. 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Write the role of one person whose work you have spoken up for lately. If nobody comes to mind, write that down too.

Go deeper

Sponsorship with a light touch

Giving advocacy has its own craft: vouch for the work, not the whole person. 'I have seen their process mapping and it was excellent' is spendable and survives a miss; 'they are amazing' is a blank check. Match the size of your claim to what you have actually seen, name the evidence, and let the decision-maker do the rest. Small accurate claims, repeated over time, build more careers than one grand endorsement.

On your own

  1. Pick one colleague, role only, and write the one-sentence vouch: the specific work you saw and what it tells a decision-maker.
  2. Identify the next room or thread where that sentence would count, and decide now that you will say it there.

The core idea

Show your work to someone senior, then ask them to say your name in a specific room.
