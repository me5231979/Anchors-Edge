05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If Someone worries the profile data will be used against them: Answer plainly and without hedging: the profile is not a performance tool and reviews never read it. It matches skills to opportunities. Skills and roles, never ratings.
If Someone says their job has no skills worth listing: Ask the room what that person's colleagues come to them for. Every role carries named skills; that question usually surfaces two inside a minute.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Is this system going to be used to cut jobs or monitor productivity?

No. The Talent Marketplace reads the skills profile you keep, nothing else: not email, not activity, not output. Its purpose under Goal 1 is retention and internal mobility, which is the opposite of a cut list. A visible skill makes you harder to overlook, not easier to replace.

If I make my skills visible, will my manager think I am trying to leave?

A current profile is the university norm, not a leaving signal; this month's Manager Monday session asks managers to make talent visible on their own teams for the same reason. Internal mobility keeps people at Vanderbilt. The riskier move is staying invisible.

I am not sure what counts as a skill worth listing. Where do I start?

Start with what people come to you for. If colleagues bring you their scheduling messes or their unreadable drafts, those are named skills. The Know-Your-Team lab and the Talent Management course on LinkedIn Learning both help you name more, if you want the extra rep.

Section 01 · The System

The university is learning to see talent.

The Talent Marketplace is Vanderbilt's internal talent map: skills profiles on one side, university needs and internal opportunities on the other. It watches nothing and grades nobody. It reads what you choose to share, and it matches.

What it actually is

The Talent Marketplace is a talent map. Input: the skills profile you keep. Output: matches between what you can do and what the university needs, from short projects to open roles. The map is only as true as the profiles that feed it.

In the room

Most workplace systems read you without asking: badge swipes, ticket queues, calendars. The Talent Marketplace is built the other way. It knows only what you tell it, so your profile is the whole of your presence on the map. The honest question for this room is what yours says right now.

Talk it through: When you last touched your skills profile, what was true about your job? What has changed since then that the Marketplace still cannot see?

Quick check: what does the Talent Marketplace actually read?

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: what is one skill you have that nobody outside your own team knows about? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Write down one skill you have that nobody outside your own team knows about.

Go deeper

Where the Marketplace ends and other systems begin

The Marketplace sits alongside systems you already know, and the division of labor matters. Oracle holds the record of your job: title, goals, reviews. The Marketplace holds your capability: what you can do and at what scale, whether or not the job asks for it. Nothing flows from reviews into the map. When a match grows into a real move, the Transfer Portal takes over; that side gets its own month later in the series.

On your own

  1. Open your skills profile next to your Oracle job record. Mark every profile line that just restates the job record; those are your rewrite targets.
  2. Write down two skills you use that appear in neither place. That gap is exactly what this week's update should close.

Section 02 · The Payoff

Opportunities start finding you.

Today, internal moves mostly go to whoever happened to hear about them. With a current profile, the search runs the other way: a project, a stretch role, or a committee that needs your skill can find you, whether or not you know the right people.

Play · What just happened?

Why this matters: Three small moments with the system. Read each one and call what it is.

Breakout · 3 min: Pairs: name one internal opportunity you heard about too late. What skill of yours would have matched it?

On your own: Recall one internal opportunity you heard about too late. What skill of yours would have matched it?

Go deeper

How a match actually finds you

A match starts as a search someone runs under pressure: a symposium is short a logistics lead, a grant needs a budget review by Friday. Searches like that use plain, concrete words, and they reward specificity and recency. 'Event logistics, groups of 200 plus, updated last month' surfaces; a bare 'communications' sinks in a pile of similar profiles. Write for the person searching in a hurry, not for a careers page.

On your own

  1. Imagine the search you would most like to be found by. Write down the exact words that searcher would type.
  2. Check whether those words, or close cousins, appear anywhere on your profile today. If not, that is your first new line.

Section 03 · The Craft

Name the skill, not the duty.

The map can only match what it can read. 'Supports departmental events' is a duty; 'event logistics for groups of 200 plus' is a skill someone can search for. This section is the craft of writing yourself legibly, and the small habit that keeps it true.

Practice · Write yourself onto the map

Why this matters: Take one thing you are genuinely good at. Choose how you write it, and how you keep the line alive.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

The anatomy of a line the map can match

A legible line has three parts: the named skill, the scale it runs at, and a hint of evidence. 'Plain-language writing' is a skill; 'for a 3,000-reader newsletter' is scale; 'monthly since 2024' is evidence it is current. You rarely need all three, but skill plus one other beats skill alone every time. When you doubt whether something counts, run the borrow test: if a colleague would borrow you for it, it earns a line.

On your own

  1. Grade your three strongest profile lines: skill named? scale shown? evidence hinted? Rewrite the weakest one now.
  2. Run the borrow test on your last two weeks: what did anyone borrow you for? Add the best answer as a new line.
Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Teach the craft of naming skills instead of duties, and the habit that keeps a profile current.

Say

“Most profiles fail quietly for one reason: they are written like job descriptions. 'Supports departmental events' tells the map nothing. 'Event logistics for groups of 200 plus' is a skill someone can search for and find. We are going to write one line the legible way, then set the habit that keeps it true.”

Do
  1. Run Write yourself onto the map; give the room two minutes to make both choices and run it.
  2. Ask one volunteer to read their outcome aloud, weak or strong.
  3. Point at the pattern: named skill, real scale, fresh update.
Ask

“Why do adjectives like dedicated or detail-oriented do nothing on a profile?”

Expect:

  • Everyone claims them
  • There is nothing to search or match on

Respond: Both right. A matching system, and honestly a busy human too, can only work with named skills. Kind words about yourself are not data.

Debrief

Legible means named, specific, and current: the skill inside the duty, in words someone would actually search.

Watch for: People underselling themselves. If someone says they have no skills worth listing, ask what colleagues come to them for; that answer is the profile line.
