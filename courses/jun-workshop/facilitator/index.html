05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If The projector or room screen fails: The lab survives it: everything runs on paper and learner devices via the welcome QR. Read the cards aloud from your own screen and keep the builds moving.
If A manager arrives without their exposure map or skills notes: The blank templates rebuild the essentials fast: five recurring tasks, three strengths, one gap. A rough fresh map beats a polished absent one, and the table test works on either.
If A manager names a real employee while testing a plan: Protect them warmly and immediately: 'Hold that. Roles, never names.' The plan works on work; the person hears it first in private, which is exactly what the plan schedules.
If End-of-year emotion surfaces at the tables: Let it breathe; a capstone is allowed to feel like one. Invite one-line reflections on the year at the close, take a few aloud, and land the room on the plans going home in warm hands.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

My plan needs budget or an HR process I don't control. Is it stuck?

Then the ask is step one, owned and dated like everything else. Two checkable lines with owners are exactly what gets funded and fast-tracked; fog is what gets deferred. Book your HR consultant the way you booked the investment conversation, plan in hand.

What if the person whose work changes doesn't want the reskill?

That answer is data, and it usually points at redeploy: the strength is real even when the appetite isn't. Hold the conversation as a genuine question, and let the Talent Marketplace surface options you can't see from inside one team. A move chosen lands better than a move assigned, every time.

The series year is ending. Who checks whether these plans actually happen?

You built the answer today: an owner per step, a peer check-in with this table, and a team that hears the plan early. The pulse follows in a week, the dashboard stays open, and the rhythm returns with room to grow. The plan reports to its owners now, which was the year's whole lesson.

Section 01 · The Overlay

Lay both maps side by side.

Spread both artifacts: the exposure map of work that is changing, and the skills read of who could grow. The plan lives where they touch. Deloitte's skills-based research matches work to skills, never to job titles. Roles only, never names.

The overlay rule

An action-ready insight has two coordinates: a task whose shape is changing, and a strength or gap on the team it points at. One coordinate makes a slide. Two make a move you can hand an owner.

In the room

This table is holding a year of looking: the talent inventory habit from the fall, the task maps from the spring, the honest sorts in between. Most planning fails for lack of exactly what is in front of you now. In this room the raw material is done; today is for writing the moves.

Talk it through: Where do your two maps touch first? Name the task and the strength, roles only, and let the table say what move they see.

Quick check: which of these is action-ready?

Grounded in: The skills-based organization, a new model for work (Deloitte)

Breakout · 3 min: Point a neighbor at one place your maps touch: an exposed task near an underused strength, roles only. The neighbor says the move they see before you say yours.

Ask

“What surprised you, seeing both maps at once?”

Expect:

  • Strengths sitting right next to exposed tasks
  • Gaps with no strength anywhere near them
  • Some maps aged; the work changed faster than expected

Respond: All three are findings. A strength beside an exposed task is a plan; a gap with no strength nearby is also a plan, just a different verb.

Debrief

One coordinate is a slide; two are a move. Every manager here just found at least one pair.

Watch for: Names sneaking onto worksheets as the maps get personal. The rule holds hardest in the capstone: roles only, on paper and out loud.

Section 02 · The Build

Write the moves you can check.

The worksheet asks for two lines. The reskill line says who grows, toward what work, starting when. The second names a strength that moves or a role rebuilt around what stays human. Both lines end with an owner and a date.

Play · Grade the line

Why this matters: Three plan lines from tables like this one. Grade each before it goes home.

Breakout · 3 min: Write both lines, then read them to your table partner exactly as written. Anywhere they ask 'meaning what?', the line is still fog: rewrite it on the spot.

Facilitator scriptFull 11 min · Core 8

Draft the reskill line and the redeploy or redesign line, checkable, owned, and dated.

Say

“The plan is deliberately small: two lines. Line one is a reskill: who grows, toward what work, starting when. Line two is a redeploy or a redesign: a strength pointed at new work, or a role rebuilt around what stays human. If a redeploy points past your own team, that is what the Talent Marketplace and the Transfer Portal are for; the university built the doors, and your line can walk through them. Both lines end the same way: an owner and a date. If you cannot check it later, it is not written yet.”

Do
  1. Run Grade the line on learner devices first, so the holds, fog, and wrong-verb tells are shared.
  2. Quiet drafting on the worksheet; walk the room and ask 'meaning what?' at any fog.
  3. Run the partner read-aloud; rewrites happen at the table with the marker, never later.
Ask

“Which line came harder, the reskill or the redeploy and redesign?”

Expect:

  • Reskill easier; training feels familiar
  • Redesign harder; rebuilding a role feels above a manager's desk
  • Dates harder than either

Respond: Redesign feels big because it is honest. Start the line anyway; the table test will tell you whether it needs a partner in HR, and that ask becomes step one.

Debrief

Two lines, four properties each: a verb, a role, an owner, a date. The whole year fits in two sentences, which was always the point.

Watch for: Hire creeping in as a first verb. Run the order aloud: reskill, redeploy, redesign, then hire, when the first three have honestly failed.

Transition

“The lines exist. Time to let other managers bend them.”

30-min core: Core: draft the reskill line fully; the second line can leave as a labeled stub with its owner and date set at the peer check-in.

Section 03 · The Test

Test the plan out loud.

Last build of the year, out loud. Kotter's change research found efforts mostly fail in the telling, so your plan meets the table: managers asking what the owner needs and what the person whose work changes will hear. Then the first conversation gets booked.

Practice · Send the plan home

Why this matters: A plan survives on who hears it first and how its dates hold. Choose both.

Choose both steps

Grounded in: Leading change: why transformation efforts fail, John Kotter (HBR)

Breakout · 3 min: Read your two lines to the table. Listeners ask the card questions: what does the owner need, what will the person hear, what slips first? Revise on the spot.

Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Pressure-test each plan aloud at the table, then convert momentum into a booked first conversation and peer check-in.

Say

“Here is why this lab is in person: plans get strong in other people's hands. John Kotter studied over a hundred organizations trying to change, and the efforts that died usually died of undercommunication, not bad planning. So you will read your two lines to the table, exactly as written, and take the card questions: what does the owner need, what will the person whose work changes hear, what slips first. Answer, revise, and then do the bravest administrative act of the year: open your calendar and book the first conversation before you leave this room.”

Do
  1. Run Send the plan home on learner devices; the who-hears-it-first pattern sets up the read-alouds.
  2. Run the table test: each manager reads, card questions fly, revisions happen on the spot; keep tables moving so everyone gets tested.
  3. Calendars open: first conversations booked, peer check-ins traded across the table; confirm out loud as they land.
Ask

“What did the table hear in your plan that you couldn't?”

Expect:

  • The person's likely first reaction to the move
  • A date that was really a hope
  • A redesign that needs an HR partner from day one

Respond: That is the lab doing its one job. Every question you just answered is one your team and your leadership no longer get to ask first.

Debrief

Tested, revised, booked: the plan leaves this room already in motion, which is the difference between a capstone and a keepsake.

Watch for: Managers booking the leadership pitch before the investment conversation. Reorder it gently: the person whose work changes hears it first; that order is the message.
