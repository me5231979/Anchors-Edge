Begin the session

Section 01 · The Machinery

Know the three protections.

Pulse and 360 tools follow strict plumbing: names come off, results appear only when enough people answer, and leaders see the aggregate. SHRM is blunt about why that matters: people answer honestly when they trust the protections.

The protection rule

Three protections stack: names come off responses, results stay hidden below a minimum group size, and leaders see aggregates and themes. Open comments are shared word for word, so write them about the work.

In the room

Think back to the last pulse you took. Between your click and any report, three things happened: your name came off, your answers joined a pool, and a report only existed once the pool cleared the minimum size. Small groups roll up a level. The one exception: open comments travel word for word.

Talk it through: What have you heard people say about who can see survey answers, and which of the three protections answers it?

Your team has four people this cycle and the minimum reporting group is five. What happens to your answers?

Grounded in: How anonymous is that employee satisfaction survey? (SHRM)

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: what have you heard people say about who can see survey answers? True or myth? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Write one thing you have heard about who can see survey answers. Mark it true or myth.

Go deeper

What happens to open comments

Scores get the full anonymization treatment; comments get less, because they travel word for word and writing style can be recognizable. So write comments you would stand behind at their most traceable: about workload, process, and patterns in the work. That is a reason to aim well, never a reason to skip them. Verbatim comments are the most read part of any survey report, which makes one well-aimed comment your loudest voice.

On your own

  1. Draft one open comment about your current work: a pattern or a process, one concrete suggestion, no named colleagues.
  2. Reread it as if your whole department could see it. If it still feels fair and useful, it is survey-ready.
  3. Save it somewhere you will find it when the next pulse opens, so the honest version is already written.

Section 02 · The Myths

Separate the myths from the one real norm.

People soften survey answers for reasons that feel sensible: someone will trace it, one voice changes nothing, honesty gets someone in trouble. Most of that is myth the plumbing already handles; one is a real norm, and the skill is telling them apart.

Play · Myth or norm?

Why this matters: Three thoughts people have with the survey open. Call each one: protected honesty, a myth, or a real norm.

Breakout · 3 min: Pairs: which myth have you half-believed at survey time, and what does the tooling actually do? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Name the myth you have half-believed at survey time, and what the tooling actually does instead.

Go deeper

The arithmetic of one voice

The one-voice myth fails on arithmetic. Aggregates are averages of small pools: on a team of eight, your answer is an eighth of the score, and a theme gets flagged when just a handful of comments rhyme. Softened answers do damage by the same arithmetic. Every inflated score buries the signal a leader needed to see, which is how a quiet problem stays quietly funded for another year.

On your own

  1. Recall the last survey answer you softened. Write what the honest score would have been and what it might have surfaced.
  2. Decide your rule before the next pulse opens: answer as if the plumbing works, because it does.
  3. Pick the one question you always rush past, and plan to give it an extra thirty seconds next cycle.

Section 03 · The Read

Read feedback at its actual size.

Sooner or later feedback arrives about you: a 360 summary, a peer comment, a survey theme from a project you led. Sheila Heen and Douglas Stone call the skill finding the coaching in the criticism, reading past the sting for the one adjustment worth a try.

Practice · Read your results

Why this matters: A 360 summary lands in your inbox with one comment that stings. Choose your first read and your next move.

Choose both steps

Grounded in: Find the Coaching in Criticism, Sheila Heen and Douglas Stone (HBR)

Go deeper

The two-week experiment, properly run

An adjustment without a window becomes a vague resolution, and a window without a review becomes forgetting. The full loop has three parts: name the adjustment in one sentence, run it for two weeks, then spend five minutes asking what changed. Most experiments end there, absorbed into normal habit. When one changes nothing, that is a finding too: the comment was noise, and you can retire it with a clear conscience.

On your own

  1. Take one piece of feedback you have received this year and write the one adjustment it suggests.
  2. Put a 15-minute review on your calendar two weeks out; that appointment is what makes it an experiment.
  3. At the review, write one line: keep it, adjust it, or retire the comment as noise.
