05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If Someone shares a heavy personal situation live on the call: Receive it with one warm sentence, then protect them from the group: 'Thank you for trusting us with that; let's talk after.' Follow up privately and point to support resources where it fits.
If Someone objects that reading tone is mind-reading or overstepping: Agree with the caution: the skill is noticing a change and asking, never diagnosing. The check-in hands the meaning back to the person, which is the opposite of mind-reading.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Isn't this asking me to be my colleagues' therapist?

No. The move is a thirty-second check-in and one feeling-first sentence, both ordinary collegial behavior. Anything heavier gets a warm handoff toward real support, and knowing that limit is part of the skill. You are a colleague who listens well; that is the whole job.

What if I check in and I'm wrong, and it gets awkward?

A light, open check-in has a built-in exit: 'all good' ends it with no harm done. People remember being noticed kindly far longer than they remember a guess that missed. The awkwardness lives in naming feelings for people, which is exactly what the check-in avoids.

What about tone in email and chat, where there is no voice?

Baselines still work: message length, response speed, punctuation, the emoji that went missing. Read the change against that person's normal, the same rule as voice. Then move the check-in itself somewhere richer; a shift noticed in chat rarely untangles in chat.

Section 01 · The Shift

Read shifts against a baseline.

Every conversation runs on two channels: the words, and everything around them. When a chatty teammate answers in one line, or a steady voice speeds up, the second channel is broadcasting. The skill starts with noticing the change from that person's normal.

The baseline rule

Shifts only mean something against a baseline. Quiet is no signal from a quiet person; it is a loud one from a talker. Read the change from this person's normal, and hold your first guess about what it means loosely.

In the room

Set the scene for the room: a teammate who usually fills every call writes 'fine, whatever works' about a plan they built themselves. Nobody reacts, because the words are agreeable. But from this person, flatness is the loudest part of the message, and it is the part most threads never answer.

Talk it through: Whose 'fine' have you learned to distrust, and what taught you? Roles, never names. And when has your own second channel said more than your words did?

A teammate who usually fills the room writes 'fine, whatever works' about a plan they built. What matters most here?

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: what is one 'tell' you have learned to read in a colleague, a shift in tone, pace, or energy? Roles, never names.

On your own: Name one 'tell' you have learned to read in a colleague: a shift in tone, pace, or energy. Roles, never names.

Go deeper

Baselines in a hybrid week

A baseline is built from the small details of one person's normal: message length, response speed, camera habits, how often they joke, whether they push back. In hybrid work you hold a few baselines per person, one per channel. A shift in one channel while the others hold steady usually means workload. A shift across every channel at once earns a check-in. Either way it is a signal, never a diagnosis; the meaning stays theirs.

On your own

  1. Write a one-line 'normal' for two colleagues you work with weekly: tone, pace, camera, chat style. Roles, never names.
  2. For one week, log shifts against those lines instead of guessing moods. Note what the baseline changed about your read.

Section 02 · The Check-In

Ask the shift, lightly.

A noticed shift is a hypothesis. Guessing turns it into a verdict ('you're clearly upset') and ignoring lets it harden on its own. The move in between is a light, private, open check-in that lets the person tell you what is true, including 'all good'.

Play · Assume, ignore, or check in?

Why this matters: You noticed a shift. Read what happens next and call the move.

Grounded in: What Great Listeners Actually Do, Jack Zenger and Joseph Folkman (HBR, 2016)

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: say a check-in line out loud that you could actually use tomorrow. Trade lines and keep the better one.

On your own: Write one check-in line you could actually use tomorrow, in your own voice.

Go deeper

Timing, channel, and the second ask

Two quiet choices decide whether a check-in lands: where and when. Private, always; an audience turns care into a spotlight. Soon beats perfect; within a day reads as attention, three weeks late reads as an audit. Move up a channel too: a shift noticed in chat untangles better in a call. If 'all good' comes back while the shift continues, offer the door once more a few days later; twice is care, a campaign is pressure.

On your own

  1. Write two check-in lines in your own voice: one for chat, one for the start of a call. Light, private, easy to decline.
  2. Recall a check-in that landed badly on you. Name what was off: the channel, the timing, or a guess dressed as a question.
Facilitator scriptFull 11 min · Core 8

Replace assuming and ignoring with the light, private, open check-in.

Say

“A noticed shift puts three moves on the table: assume, ignore, or ask. Assuming is a guess wearing certainty, and people read your silence as an answer anyway if you ignore it. The check-in is the third move, and it matches what Jack Zenger and Joseph Folkman found when they studied thousands of leaders for HBR: the best listeners are not silent nodders, they ask questions. 'You went quiet back there, anything you'd add?' costs thirty seconds and asks instead of guessing.”

Do
  1. Run Assume, ignore, or check in? live on everyone's screens.
  2. After round one, linger: the guess was private and kind and still a guess.
  3. Run the breakout: say a check-in line aloud in your own voice.
Ask

“What makes 'no need to answer' such a strong ending for a check-in?”

Expect:

  • It removes the pressure to perform an answer
  • It makes declining safe
  • It proves the door is real

Respond: Right. An exit ramp makes honesty cheaper. Half the value of a check-in lands even when the answer is 'all good.'

Debrief

A real check-in is private and easy to decline, and it hands the meaning back to the person instead of guessing it for them.

Watch for: Scripted-sounding lines in the breakout. Push for their own voice; a borrowed phrase reads as a technique, and techniques get polite answers.

Section 03 · The Response

Answer the feeling, then the facts.

When a message arrives carrying frustration, most of us answer the content and skip the feeling, and the sender hears half a reply. Answering the feeling takes one plain sentence, and it is the sentence that makes the rest of your answer land as help.

Practice · Answer the whole message

Why this matters: A colleague writes: 'The data STILL isn't ready. I've asked three times and my report is due Friday. Whatever, I'll figure it out.' Build your reply.

Choose both steps

Grounded in: Active listening research, Center for Creative Leadership

Go deeper

Acknowledge without diagnosing

There is a line between answering a feeling and assigning one. 'Three asks with Friday looming? Genuinely frustrating' answers; 'you are angry' assigns. Keep the sentence about the situation: three asks and a deadline would frustrate anyone, and saying so labels nobody. When a message runs hot, let the content wait a beat: acknowledge, then offer a call. Keep it to one sentence; a paragraph of validation reads as performance.

On your own

  1. Find a charged message you answered facts-first. Rewrite the reply: one plain sentence for the feeling, then help with a time on it.
  2. Draft two reusable feeling-first openers about situations: one for a delay, one for being overruled in a meeting.
Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Train the feeling-first reply: one plain sentence answering the emotion before any facts or fixes.

Say

“Sometimes the second channel arrives in writing: capital letters in the middle of a message and a 'whatever, I'll figure it out' at the end. The reflex is to answer the content, because the content has an answer. A frustrated message is two messages, though, and if you only answer the facts, the person heard you ignore the other one. The Center for Creative Leadership teaches this move as reflecting: mirror back the feeling you heard before you touch the problem. One plain sentence does it, and then the facts land as help.”

Do
  1. Run Answer the whole message; two minutes for both lines, then send it.
  2. Ask one volunteer to read their outcome aloud, weak or strong.
  3. Point at the pattern: name the frustration plainly, then relief with a clock on it.
Ask

“Why does the quick apology in slot one still land badly?”

Expect:

  • It is generic; it could answer any message
  • It skips what they actually said
  • Speed without acknowledgment reads as brush-off

Respond: Yes. The apology answers that a message arrived; the strong line answers what the message carried. People can tell which one they got.

Debrief

Feeling first, one plain sentence, then concrete help with a time attached. That order is the whole skill.

Watch for: Overwrought feeling-lines that sound like a script. One plain sentence beats a paragraph of validation; this is workplace listening, kept light.
