Hear what isn't said.

Begin the session

Section 01 · The Shift

Read shifts against a baseline.

Every conversation runs on two channels: the words, and everything around them. When a chatty teammate answers in one line, or a steady voice speeds up, the second channel is broadcasting. The skill starts with noticing the change from that person's normal.

The baseline rule

Shifts only mean something against a baseline. Quiet is no signal from a quiet person; it is a loud one from a talker. Read the change from this person's normal, and hold your first guess about what it means loosely.

In the room

Set the scene for the room: a teammate who usually fills every call writes 'fine, whatever works' about a plan they built themselves. Nobody reacts, because the words are agreeable. But from this person, flatness is the loudest part of the message, and it is the part most threads never answer.

Talk it through: Whose 'fine' have you learned to distrust, and what taught you? Roles, never names. And when has your own second channel said more than your words did?

A teammate who usually fills the room writes 'fine, whatever works' about a plan they built. What matters most here?

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: what is one 'tell' you have learned to read in a colleague, a shift in tone, pace, or energy? Roles, never names.

On your own: Name one 'tell' you have learned to read in a colleague: a shift in tone, pace, or energy. Roles, never names.

Go deeper

Baselines in a hybrid week

A baseline is built from the small details of one person's normal: message length, response speed, camera habits, how often they joke, whether they push back. In hybrid work you hold a few baselines per person, one per channel. A shift in one channel while the others hold steady usually means workload. A shift across every channel at once earns a check-in. Either way it is a signal, never a diagnosis; the meaning stays theirs.

On your own

  1. Write a one-line 'normal' for two colleagues you work with weekly: tone, pace, camera, chat style. Roles, never names.
  2. For one week, log shifts against those lines instead of guessing moods. Note what the baseline changed about your read.

Section 02 · The Check-In

Ask the shift, lightly.

A noticed shift is a hypothesis. Guessing turns it into a verdict ('you're clearly upset') and ignoring lets it harden on its own. The move in between is a light, private, open check-in that lets the person tell you what is true, including 'all good'.

Play · Assume, ignore, or check in?

Why this matters: You noticed a shift. Read what happens next and call the move.

Grounded in: What Great Listeners Actually Do, Zenger and Folkman (HBR)

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: say a check-in line out loud that you could actually use tomorrow. Trade lines and keep the better one.

On your own: Write one check-in line you could actually use tomorrow, in your own voice.

Go deeper

Timing, channel, and the second ask

Two quiet choices decide whether a check-in lands: where and when. Private, always; an audience turns care into a spotlight. Soon beats perfect; within a day reads as attention, three weeks late reads as an audit. Move up a channel too: a shift noticed in chat untangles better in a call. If 'all good' comes back while the shift continues, offer the door once more a few days later; twice is care, a campaign is pressure.

On your own

  1. Write two check-in lines in your own voice: one for chat, one for the start of a call. Light, private, easy to decline.
  2. Recall a check-in that landed badly on you. Name what was off: the channel, the timing, or a guess dressed as a question.

Section 03 · The Response

Answer the feeling, then the facts.

When a message arrives carrying frustration, most of us answer the content and skip the feeling, and the sender hears half a reply. Answering the feeling takes one plain sentence, and it is the sentence that makes the rest of your answer land as help.

Practice · Answer the whole message

Why this matters: A colleague writes: 'The data STILL isn't ready. I've asked three times and my report is due Friday. Whatever, I'll figure it out.' Build your reply.

Choose both steps

Grounded in: Active listening research, Center for Creative Leadership

Go deeper

Acknowledge without diagnosing

There is a line between answering a feeling and assigning one. 'Three asks with Friday looming? Genuinely frustrating' answers; 'you are angry' assigns. Keep the sentence about the situation: three asks and a deadline would frustrate anyone, and saying so labels nobody. When a message runs hot, let the content wait a beat: acknowledge, then offer a call. Keep it to one sentence; a paragraph of validation reads as performance.

On your own

  1. Find a charged message you answered facts-first. Rewrite the reply: one plain sentence for the feeling, then help with a time on it.
  2. Draft two reusable feeling-first openers about situations: one for a delay, one for being overruled in a meeting.

The core idea

When a colleague's normal shifts, check in privately before you act on the words.
