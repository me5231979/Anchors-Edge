05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If Someone names a real colleague while describing a flight risk: Protect them warmly and immediately: 'Hold that. Roles, never names.' The read works on patterns, not on people's reputations in a group call.
If A manager says they have no time for stay interviews: Do the math out loud: one conversation is 30 minutes; one backfill is four to six months. The conversation is the cheap option.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Isn't watching for flight risk just spying on my team?

No tools, no monitoring, no message-reading. This is about patterns you already see in the normal course of managing: who volunteers, who owns, who asks about growth. The response to a signal is a conversation, which is the opposite of surveillance.

What if the stay interview surfaces a problem I can't fix, like pay?

Name what you control and what you don't, honestly. In Gallup's exit research, manager attention and growth drive plenty of preventable departures, and both of those are yours. For the rest, carry it upward visibly; being advocated for retains people too.

What do I do with the skills inventory once I have it?

Three uses this month: match one hidden skill to one real task, feed one growth ask, and hold one conversation. Later months connect it to the Talent Marketplace and the Transfer Portal, where the inventory becomes the university's map, not just yours.

Section 01 · The Inventory

Inventory skills, not titles.

A skills inventory records what each person can do, which is always more than the role requires. Ask one question per person: what do they do well that the job never asks for? The analyst who writes well and the coordinator who de-escalates both belong on it.

The inventory question

For each person, ask: what do they do well that the role does not require? Answers to that question are where succession plans, stretch projects, and internal moves come from.

In the room

Think about the last person you hired. You read a resume full of skills, and then the org chart reduced them to one title. Every team on this call carries skills that came up in interviews and have never been used since. The inventory question recovers what the title erased.

Talk it through: Whose resume surprised you when you first read it? What did they bring that their current role never touches, and what would it take to finally use it?

Quick check: which of these belongs in a skills inventory?

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: name one hidden skill someone on your team has that their job never uses. One pair shares.

On your own: Write down one hidden skill someone on your team has that their job never uses.

Go deeper

Three sources feed one honest inventory

Your own observation is the weakest source, because it only samples the skills the current workload happens to exercise. Triangulate three: what you have watched them do, what colleagues borrow them for, and what they say when asked directly. The third source is where the surprises live. Most people carry a skill from a past role or a side interest nobody has asked about, and will name it the first time someone does.

On your own

  1. Pick one person on your team and write three lines, one per source: seen, borrowed, self-reported. Leave the third blank until you have actually asked them.
  2. Reread the resume or Oracle record they were hired with. Note one skill it mentions that the role has never used.
  3. Bring the inventory line to your next 1:1 and let them correct it; their edits are the most accurate data you will get.

Section 02 · The Signals

Sort flight-risk signals from noise.

Gallup finds 42 percent of people who quit say their manager could have done something, and nearly half heard no question about their future in their last three months. In this section you practice sorting real signals from busy-season noise.

Play · Signal or noise?

Why this matters: Read each situation and call it. Judge the change against the person's own baseline: a real signal earns a conversation this week, and noise mostly needs watching.

Grounded in: Preventable turnover research (Gallup, 2024)

Breakout · 3 min: Breakout pairs: which signal on your team have you been calling noise? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Name one signal on your own team you may have been calling noise.

Go deeper

Read against their baseline, not the team's

The common mistake is comparing a person to the rest of the team: your quietest analyst looks fine next to your loudest coordinator. Flight-risk signals only mean anything against a person's own baseline, which is why long-tenured managers miss them; the baseline drifted slowly. The fix is a small discipline: know what normal ownership looks like for each person, then notice departures from that, in either direction.

On your own

  1. Write one sentence per team member describing their normal: what they volunteer for, what they own without being asked.
  2. Flag anyone whose last month departs from their own sentence; treat the flags as things to check, never verdicts.
  3. For one flagged person, book a light check-in this week. The conversation is how you test the read.

Do
  1. Run Signal or noise? live; everyone plays on their own screen.
  2. After round two, pause: ask who called it noise and why. The disagreement is the teaching.
  3. Run the breakout on the signal they have been calling noise.
Ask

“What makes the growth ask in round three so easy to miss?”

Expect:

  • It sounds like a budget question
  • It arrives when you are busy
  • It is flattering, so it feels safe to postpone

Respond: All true. The reframe: a growth ask is talent telling you the retention lever. Postponing it is declining it.

Debrief

Signals are pattern changes around ownership and growth. Noise is workload weather. When unsure, one light conversation settles it.

Watch for: Anyone treating this as monitoring individuals. Frame stays on patterns you observe in the normal course of managing, never on surveillance.

Transition

“You have the read. Now it has to earn a conversation.”

Section 03 · The Move

Turn the read into a conversation.

A talent read pays off when you act on it. SHRM's stay interview guidance runs on five questions and one discipline: ask, listen, then act on what you heard. In this lab you build the opening and the question you would actually use.

Practice · Build the conversation

Why this matters: Pick the person your read points to. Choose how you open and what you ask, then run it; a specific opening and a forward-looking question are what change the answers.

Choose both steps

Grounded in: How to conduct stay interviews: five key questions (SHRM)

Go deeper

The follow-through is the retention

The conversation itself retains nobody; the follow-through does. Whatever you learn creates a small debt, a stretch task to find or a course to fund. Pay it visibly within two weeks, even partially, and the person learns that talking to you moves things. Miss it, and your next stay interview earns exactly the polite yes it was designed to avoid.

On your own

  1. After the conversation, write one line: the skill or want you heard, and the smallest visible action it points to.
  2. Take the action within two weeks: forward the course link, propose the stretch task, or update your inventory and say so.
  3. At the following 1:1, name what you did with what they told you. Closing that loop is what makes the next conversation honest.
