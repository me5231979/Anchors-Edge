Section 01 · The Inventory

Titles tell you the job. Not the talent.

Every team carries skills that never appear on an org chart: the analyst who writes beautifully, the coordinator who de-escalates anyone. A talent read starts by listing what people can do, not what they were hired to do.

The inventory question

For each person, ask: what do they do well that the role does not require? Answers to that question are where succession plans, stretch projects, and internal moves come from.

Quick check: which of these belongs in a skills inventory?

On your own: Write down one hidden skill someone on your team has that their job never uses.

Go deeper

Three sources feed one honest inventory

Your own observation is the weakest source, because it only samples the skills the current workload happens to exercise. Triangulate three: what you have watched them do, what colleagues borrow them for, and what they say when asked directly. The third source is where the surprises live. Most people carry a skill from a past role or a side interest nobody has asked about, and will name it the first time someone does.

On your own

  1. Pick one person on your team and write three lines, one per source: seen, borrowed, self-reported. Leave the third blank until you have actually asked them.
  2. Reread the resume or Oracle record they were hired with. Note one skill it mentions that the role has never used.
  3. Bring the inventory line to your next 1:1 and let them correct it; their edits are the most accurate data you will get.

Section 02 · The Signals

Flight risk has a sound.

People rarely announce they are leaving. They go quiet in meetings they used to own, stop volunteering, or ask questions about growth and get no good answer. The skill is telling those signals apart from ordinary busy-season noise.

Play · Signal or noise?

Why this matters: Five second calls decide whether you act early or write the farewell card. Read each situation and call it.

On your own: Name one signal on your own team you may have been calling noise.

Go deeper

Read against their baseline, not the team's

The common mistake is comparing a person to the rest of the team: your quietest analyst looks fine next to your loudest coordinator. Flight-risk signals only mean anything against a person's own baseline, which is why long-tenured managers miss them; the baseline drifted slowly. The fix is a small discipline: know what normal ownership looks like for each person, then notice departures from that, in either direction.

On your own

  1. Write one sentence per team member describing their normal: what they volunteer for, what they own without being asked.
  2. Flag anyone whose last month departs from their own sentence. That is a watch list, not a verdict.
  3. For one flagged person, book a light check-in this week. The conversation is how you test the read.

Section 03 · The Move

The read earns a conversation.

A talent read that stays in your head changes nothing. It becomes retention the moment you hold the conversation: a stay interview, a growth check-in, or a skills ask. Build the opening you would actually use.

Practice · Build the conversation

Why this matters: One person on your team deserves this talk first. Choose how you open it and what you ask.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

The follow-through is the retention

The conversation itself retains nobody; the follow-through does. Whatever you learn creates a small debt: a stretch task to find, a course to fund, a hidden skill to write into your inventory. Pay it visibly within two weeks, even partially, and the person learns that talking to you moves things. Miss it, and your next stay interview earns exactly the polite yes it was designed to avoid.

On your own

  1. After the conversation, write one line: the skill or want you heard, and the smallest visible action it points to.
  2. Take the action within two weeks: forward the course link, propose the stretch task, or update your inventory and say so.
  3. At the following 1:1, name what you did with what they told you. The closed loop is the retention lever.

The core idea

You cannot retain, grow, or move talent you have never seen.
