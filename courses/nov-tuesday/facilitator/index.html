05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If Someone asks a Portal question you cannot answer, like a specific stage or policy: Say so plainly, capture it in chat, tag it for the navigators, and send the answer in the 7-day pulse. Modeling 'ask a navigator' is itself the lesson.
If Someone shares a story of a manager who blocked an internal move: Thank them for the honesty, restate the norm without debating their case, and point to Monday's sending-manager session as the fix in motion. Route the specific case to HR offline.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Will my manager see that I expressed interest?

Assume yes, and lead with it: the design expectation is that you tell them yourself first. Managers here are trained as sending managers; the conversation is usually easier than the one you are imagining, and a navigator can help you plan it.

Is expressing interest a commitment to take the role?

No. It opens a conversation, and you can step back at any stage. Treat it like raising your hand in a meeting: it means 'tell me more,' and plenty of strong moves start with three raised hands and one real conversation.

I like my job. Why should I bother with a profile?

A current profile serves people who are staying too: it is how project leads find you for gigs, how your skills get seen beyond your team, and how the university plans. Opportunity finding you costs nothing; being invisible might.

Section 01 · The Profile

Your profile is how opportunity finds you.

The Portal matches people to opportunity through skills. A profile that only repeats your job title keeps most of your range invisible, and ten minutes of honest listing fixes that.

The profile rule

List what you can do, wherever you learned it, including the skills your current role never asks for. The Portal matches on skills, so every one you leave off is a door that stays shut. What you want to build next belongs on there too.

In the room

Last month's theme was seeing the talent around you; your profile is how your talent gets seen. The stakes run past any one posting. LinkedIn's Workplace Learning Report found people stay nearly twice as long at organizations that do internal mobility well, and mobility starts at the profile.

Talk it through: Why do so many of our strongest skills never make it into a job description? What would change for you if the rest of the university could see yours?

Which profile gets found by the right opportunities?

Grounded in: Internal mobility and retention (LinkedIn Workplace Learning)

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: name one skill you use every week that your job title would never suggest. One pair shares.

On your own: Write down one skill you use every week that your job title would never suggest.

Go deeper

Writing skills the Portal can match

There is a craft to naming a skill. 'Good with people' matches nothing; 'onboarding new hires' or 'de-escalating frustrated callers' matches real postings. Name the activity and keep it specific, splitting big skills into the pieces you actually do. Then add what you want to build next: that short list is how a stretch gig or a navigator finds you before you knew to look.

On your own

  1. Draft ten skills as activities: what you do, for whom, with what. The hidden ones show up after the first five.
  2. Pick two skills you want to grow next and add them to your profile as the direction you are building.

Section 02 · The Search

Search by the skills you want to use.

The Portal lists projects and short gigs alongside jobs. The World Economic Forum expects 39 percent of key skills to change by 2030, so search by the skills you want to use rather than your title.

Practice · Build your first pass

Why this matters: Fifteen minutes in the Portal this week. Choose how you search and how you express interest.

Choose both steps

Grounded in: Future of Jobs Report 2025, skills outlook (WEF)

Go deeper

The case for a six-week gig

A gig deserves more respect than it gets. Six weeks on another team answers questions no posting can: whether you enjoy the work daily, whether the team's rhythm suits you, whether the skill belongs at the center of your job. It also leaves evidence, a finished project and a manager in another building who has seen your work. Some of the strongest internal moves start as a gig both sides wanted to continue.

On your own

  1. Run one skill-first search filtered to gigs and short projects. Save anything that pulls at you, even slightly.
  2. For your favorite, write the question a navigator could answer about fitting the time alongside your current role.
Facilitator scriptFull 11 min · Core 8

Teach skill-first search across roles, gigs, and projects, and the open way to express interest.

Say

“Now the search. The instinct is to look for your own job title in other buildings, and that finds you the job you already have. There is a second reason titles are a weak search term. The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report has employers expecting 39 percent of the skills a job needs to change by 2030, so the title stays put while the work underneath it moves. Search instead by the skills you want to use more. And notice the Portal lists gigs and short projects alongside jobs; a six-week gig is a low-risk way to test a direction. Then, when something pulls at you, raise your hand in the open.”

Do
  1. Run Build your first pass; two minutes to make both choices and run it.
  2. Ask a volunteer to read their outcome aloud, weak or strong.
  3. Point at the pattern: skill-first search, open hand.
Ask

“Why do gigs and projects matter so much for a first Portal move?”

Expect:

  • Lower commitment than a full move
  • A way to test a direction
  • A way to be seen by another team

Respond: A gig is a working interview in both directions, and it builds the exact evidence your profile needs for the bigger move later.

Debrief

Search by skill, include gigs, raise your hand where people can see it. That is the whole method.

Watch for: Anyone treating express-interest as a binding commitment. It opens a conversation; it signs nothing.

Section 03 · In the Open

No secrecy required.

The old instinct says job searching means hiding. The Portal is built for the opposite: managers are expected to support moves, navigators exist to help, and every stage has a next step you can ask about. Read each situation and call the move.

Play · Call the move

Why this matters: Three situations from the weeks after you raise your hand. Call each one.

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: which old job-search habit would be hardest for you to drop? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Name the old job-search habit that would be hardest for you to drop.

Go deeper

What happens after you raise your hand

After you express interest, the hiring team sees your profile and your raised hand, and the process moves through stages you are allowed to ask about: review, conversations, decision. None of it is secret and none of it is binding. Navigators sit outside the hiring team, so they can tell you the stage, coach your skills profile, and help you plan the manager conversation, with nothing counted against you for asking.

On your own

  1. Book 20 minutes with a navigator this month, even without a live application. Ask what strong first moves look like.
  2. Write the sentence you would use to tell your manager you expressed interest, and say it out loud once.
