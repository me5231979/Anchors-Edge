Begin the session

Section 01 · The Gap

You are the last to see your own gap.

Nobody experiences your intentions; they experience your impact, and the distance between the two hides from you by definition. Tasha Eurich's research puts it plainly: 95 percent of us believe we see ourselves clearly, and only 10 to 15 percent actually do.

The blind spot rule

Intent is what you meant; impact is what happened to the other person. You have direct access to only one of these, so your self-view is always missing data. The fix is borrowed eyes: someone who saw the impact and will tell you about it.

In the room

Think of the last email you reread before sending. You checked it against your intent, the one thing you cannot ship. The receiver got tone, timing, and a history you never saw. That asymmetry runs both directions all day, in meetings, handoffs, and chat, which is why nobody's self-view is complete.

Talk it through: Where in your work does your intent most often get misread, and who actually saw how it landed?

Why can't you find your own blind spot by reflecting harder?

Grounded in: Tasha Eurich, What Self-Awareness Really Is (HBR)

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: recall a time your words landed differently than you meant. What did the gap turn out to be? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Recall one time your words landed differently than you meant. Write what the gap turned out to be.

Go deeper

Why the gap grows with influence

Eurich's team found that experience and power hinder self-awareness: the more senior people get, the less unprompted truth they hear, since everyone edits harder for someone who can affect their week. Blind spots grow at the pace candor shrinks, so waiting to be told rarely works. The likeliest people to see your gap are the ones least obliged to flatter you: peers on shared projects, partners in other units.

On your own

  1. List three people who see your work's impact up close. Star the one least obliged to flatter you.
  2. Write one recent moment where their read of you might differ from yours. That moment is raw material for the ask you build in section two.

Section 02 · The Ask

Make the ask small and specific.

Ask a colleague 'do you have any feedback for me?' and you will get kindness and nothing. Truth comes from a different shape of ask: one trusted person, one specific situation, one honest question, and a clear sign you can take the answer.

Practice · Build the ask

Why this matters: One colleague sees your work up close. Choose who you ask and how you ask, and see what truth comes back.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

Timing the ask so truth can land

Even a perfect ask fails at the wrong moment. Truth needs privacy, a little slack, and a fresh memory: ask one on one, a day or two after the moment, never in front of an audience or at the tail end of a brutal week. Priming helps more than people expect. Telling your colleague the day before that you will ask gives an honest person time to find kind words for a true thing, which is when the best answers arrive.

On your own

  1. Pick the moment you want the truth about and note when it happened. If it is more than two weeks old, choose a fresher one.
  2. Schedule the ask: a private ten minutes within two days of the moment or your next shared piece of work.
  3. Send the priming line the day before: tomorrow I want one honest sentence about how X landed, and brief is fine.

Section 03 · The Receipt

Thank them, adjust once, then stop.

Stone and Heen name what makes honesty expensive: the identity trigger, when one sentence about a handoff starts reading as a verdict on who you are. The sustainable receipt stays small. Thank them, adjust once, stop.

Play · Call the receipt

Why this matters: Three colleagues hear one honest sentence about their impact. Call each response: a clean receipt, a deflection, or a spiral.

Grounded in: Thanks for the Feedback, Stone and Heen (Penguin Random House)

Breakout · 3 min: Pairs: what does your version of the spiral look like after hard feedback, and what would the full stop look like? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Describe your version of the spiral after hard feedback, then write what the full stop would look like instead.

Go deeper

What to do with the wince

The wince is physiology, not a verdict, and it passes in about ninety seconds if you do not feed it a story. So receive in two beats. In the moment, just thank them; the analysis can wait an hour. Later, write the feedback as one neutral sentence about behavior, never character: my emails read as curt, rather than I am cold. Behavior sentences suggest adjustments; character sentences only suggest shame.

On your own

  1. Write down the hardest feedback you remember receiving, phrased the way it felt: as a character verdict.
  2. Rewrite it as one neutral behavior sentence, then note the single adjustment it points to.
  3. Rehearse your two-beat script aloud once: thank you, that is useful, let me sit with it.

Self-awareness: closing your blind spots.

2 · Make the ask small and specific.

A real answer. Specific, kind, and slightly uncomfortable, which is how truth usually dresses.

Grounded in: Thanks for the Feedback, Stone and Heen (Penguin Random House)

The recap, with the answers

1 · Why does everyone have a blind spot?

Answer: You experience your intent; others experience your impact

The gap between intent and impact hides from you by definition. The missing data lives in other people's experience, so borrowed eyes are required.

2 · Which ask is most likely to get you the truth?

Answer: One specific moment, one honest question, to one trusted colleague

Small and specific is answerable. One situation, one question, and permission to be brief makes honesty cheap enough for a colleague to hand over.

3 · The clean receipt for hard feedback is...

Answer: A thank you, one adjustment, and a full stop

Small receipts are repeatable. Thank them, make one adjustment, and stop; that keeps the price of truth low enough that you will ask again.

Your commitment

Pick the person who sees your work up close. Role only, never a name; nothing you type is saved or sent.

  • The moment ask. Pick one recent moment and ask how it landed: one situation, one honest sentence, permission to be brief.
  • The pattern ask. Ask what one habit of yours they would adjust if they were you, and take the answer at face value.
  • The follow-up ask. Return to feedback you once brushed off and ask them to say the honest version again, this time to a ready listener.
