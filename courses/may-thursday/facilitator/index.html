05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If Someone names a real colleague while describing feedback that hurt: Protect them warmly and immediately: 'Hold that. Roles, never names.' The skill works on patterns and asks, never on individual reputations in a group call.
If A learner shares something raw and the room goes heavy: Honor it briefly, thank them for the trust, and offer a follow-up after the session. Then return the room to the skill; depth belongs in a 1:1, and the session keeps its pace.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

What if the honest answer I get is wrong about me?

It can be, and it is still data: it is how you landed for that person in that moment. You owe it a weighing, never an overhaul. Keep the fraction that rings true, and remember one report of impact is one angle, never a census.

Isn't asking for feedback like this just fishing for compliments?

The shape prevents that. A compliment-fisher asks vague questions in public; this ask is private, specific, and explicitly invites the uncomfortable answer. Colleagues can tell the difference immediately, and they respect the real version.

I'm not a manager, so isn't this month's measurement about other people?

The measurement applies to managers; the muscle applies to everyone. Careers at every level run on impact, and people who can collect honest signal about theirs grow faster, whatever their title. Your blind spot does not care about your org chart.

Section 01 · The Gap

You are the last to see your own gap.

Nobody experiences your intentions; they experience your impact. The distance between the two is your blind spot, and it hides from you by definition: you were there for the intent and absent for the impact. The self-aware just know where to look.

The blind spot rule

Intent is what you meant; impact is what happened to the other person. You have direct access to only one of these, so your self-view is always missing data. The fix is borrowed eyes: someone who saw the impact and will tell you about it.

In the room

Think of the last email you reread before sending. You checked it against your intent, the one thing you cannot ship. The receiver got tone, timing, and a history you never saw. That asymmetry runs both directions all day, in meetings, handoffs, and chat, which is why nobody's self-view is complete.

Talk it through: Where in your work does your intent most often get misread, and who actually saw how it landed?

Why can't you find your own blind spot by reflecting harder?

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: recall a time your words landed differently than you meant. What did the gap turn out to be? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Recall one time your words landed differently than you meant. Write what the gap turned out to be.

Go deeper

Why the gap grows with influence

The more influence you have, the less unprompted truth you hear: people edit harder for anyone who can affect their week. So blind spots quietly grow at exactly the pace candor shrinks, which is why waiting to be told rarely works. Whatever your role, the people most likely to see your gap clearly are the ones closest to the work and least obliged to flatter you: peers on shared projects, partners in other units.

On your own

  1. List three people who see your work's impact up close. Star the one least obliged to flatter you.
  2. Write one recent moment where their read of you might differ from yours. That moment is raw material for the ask you build in section two.

Section 02 · The Ask

Truth arrives when the ask is small.

Ask a colleague 'do you have any feedback for me?' and you will get kindness and nothing. Truth comes from a different shape of ask: one trusted person, one specific situation, one honest question, and a clear sign you can take the answer.

Practice · Build the ask

Why this matters: One colleague sees your work up close. Choose who you ask and how you ask, and see what truth comes back.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

Timing the ask so truth can land

Even a perfect ask fails at the wrong moment. Truth needs privacy, a little slack, and a fresh memory: ask one on one, a day or two after the moment, never in front of an audience or at the tail end of a brutal week. Priming helps more than people expect. Telling your colleague the day before that you will ask gives an honest person time to find kind words for a true thing, which is when the best answers arrive.

On your own

  1. Pick the moment you want the truth about and note when it happened. If it is more than two weeks old, choose a fresher one.
  2. Schedule the ask: a private ten minutes within two days of the moment or your next shared piece of work.
  3. Send the priming line the day before: tomorrow I want one honest sentence about how X landed, and brief is fine.

Teach the small, specific ask that makes honesty cheap enough for a colleague to give.

Say

“Here is why most of us have gotten so little useful feedback in our careers: the asks were too big. 'Any feedback for me?' asks a colleague to gamble the relationship on an essay. The ask that works is small. One trusted person who has seen your work. One specific moment. One honest sentence, with permission to be brief. Do

  2. Ask one volunteer to read their outcome aloud, weak or strong.
  3. Name the pattern: witnessed impact, small scope, permission to be brief.
Ask

“What makes 'be brutally honest with me' fail?”

Expect:

  • It is too big to answer
  • Nobody quite believes you mean it

Respond: Both. Brutal invitations still get soft answers because the job is impossible. Shrink the ask and the honesty shows up on its own.

Debrief

Truth-tellers need two credentials: they saw the impact, and they have been straight with you before. The ask needs one moment and permission to be brief.

Watch for: Learners choosing their closest work friend by default. Warmth is lovely; the credential is honesty plus proximity to the work.

Transition

“The ask will work. Which means you are about to hear something true, so let us prepare the landing.”

Section 03 · The Receipt

Receive it like data, at arm's length.

The reason people stop asking for truth is the cost of hearing it. The sustainable receipt is small on purpose: one thank you, one adjustment, and a full stop, with no week-long spiral attached. Cheap receipts are what make asking again possible.

Play · Call the receipt

Why this matters: Three colleagues hear one honest sentence about their impact. Call each response: a clean receipt, a deflection, or a spiral.

Breakout · 3 min: Pairs: what does your version of the spiral look like after hard feedback, and what would the full stop look like? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Describe your version of the spiral after hard feedback, then write what the full stop would look like instead.

Go deeper

What to do with the wince

The wince is physiology, not a verdict, and it passes in about ninety seconds if you do not feed it a story. So receive in two beats. In the moment, just thank them; the analysis can wait an hour. Later, write the feedback as one neutral sentence about behavior, never character: my emails read as curt, rather than I am cold. Behavior sentences suggest adjustments; character sentences only suggest shame.

On your own

  1. Write down the hardest feedback you remember receiving, phrased the way it felt: as a character verdict.
  2. Rewrite it as one neutral behavior sentence, then note the single adjustment it points to.
  3. Rehearse your two-beat script aloud once: thank you, that is useful, let me sit with it.
Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Install the clean receipt: one thank you, one adjustment, a full stop, so asking stays affordable.

Say

“Last piece, and it is the one that keeps the loop alive. The reason most of us quietly stop asking for truth is that hearing it costs too much: the deflection costs the relationship, and the spiral costs the week. The clean receipt is deliberately small. Thank them once, because they took a risk for you. Make one adjustment, because that is what the sentence was for. Then full stop. If the receipt is cheap, you will ask again, and asking again is the whole game.”

Do
  1. Run Call the receipt live on learner screens.
  2. After round three, pause on the spiral: ask why over-absorbing feels virtuous.
  3. Run the breakout on spirals and full stops.
Ask

“Why does the spiral feel like taking feedback seriously?”

Expect:

  • Suffering feels like accountability
  • It proves we care about the work

Respond: Right, and it is counterfeit accountability. The colleague offered one adjustment; the spiral delivers a crisis instead and quietly punishes them for honesty.

Debrief

Deflection underpays for truth, the spiral overpays, and the clean receipt pays exactly what it costs: thanks, one adjustment, done.

Watch for: Anyone processing real, raw feedback live. Offer the frame gently and follow up after; the room stays a skills session.
