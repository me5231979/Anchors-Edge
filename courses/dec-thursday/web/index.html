Clarity that sticks.

Most dropped balls were never caught to begin with: two people left the same conversation carrying different versions of it. Clarity is a skill, and it has three moves.

Section 01 · The Ask

Vague requests deserve better questions.

Make it better. Own this going forward. Requests like that dissolve by Tuesday. Gallup calls knowing what is expected of you the most basic employee need; three questions secure it.

The three clarity questions

Before accepting any significant task, get answers to three things: what does done look like, when is it actually needed, and who makes the final call? Asking them is a service to the requester; every one you skip becomes a guess you own.

Quick check: asking your manager what done looks like is...

Grounded in: Gallup Q12: clarity of expectations

On your own: Name one current task where you cannot describe what done looks like. Write the clarity question you will ask, and to whom.

Go deeper

When the requester does not know yet

Sometimes the honest answer to what does done look like is: I do not know yet. That answer is useful too. It tells you the task is exploratory, so agree on a checkpoint instead of a deliverable: I will bring a rough version Thursday and we will look at it together. And when the asker is senior, bundle the three questions into one sentence that reads as momentum: so I aim this right, what does done look like and by when?

On your own

  1. Write the bundled one-sentence version of the three questions in your own voice, one you could say to the most senior person you work with.
  2. Pick one live task with no picture of done. Decide whether it needs the three questions or a checkpoint agreement, then send the ask today.

Section 02 · The Restate

The restate finds gaps while they are cheap.

Restating an expectation in your own words is the cheapest test it will ever get. A match buys you confidence, and a mismatch finds the gap while fixing it still costs minutes. The judgment call is knowing which handoffs need it.

Play · Clear enough?

Why this matters: Three handoffs from a normal week. Call whether you can act, need to say it back, or need it in writing.

On your own: Name a live handoff nobody has said back yet. Write your one-sentence restate of it.

Go deeper

Restating up, down, and sideways

The restate flexes with the relationship. With a peer it can be casual: so I take the data pull and you take the writeup, right? With someone senior, attach it to your next step so it reads as motion: I will start with the survey data, since done means the summary by Friday. And when a handoff has more than three moving parts, restate in writing; a short list is easier to correct than a paragraph of talk.

On your own

  1. Take the live handoff from your solo prompt and write two restates: the casual peer version and the one you would send someone senior.
  2. Use one of them in the real conversation this week. Whatever correction comes back is rework you just saved.

Section 03 · The Close

Agreements survive in writing.

The last move happens after the conversation: a short written line, same day, saying what you heard and what happens next. Ninety seconds of typing is the difference between an agreement and a memory. Build the close you would actually send.

Practice · Write the close

Why this matters: You just left a planning conversation with real commitments in it. Choose how you restate and how you close the loop.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

Anatomy of a close that settles arguments

A working close reads like this: Thanks for the planning talk. What I heard: full draft to you by the 15th, I own the data section, you own the intro. Flag anything I got off. Notice the last line. The close is a test, and the best result is a correction within the hour, because that reply just saved you the rework. Silence becomes agreement you can point to; a correction is a gap caught while it costs nothing.

On your own

  1. Build your reusable three-line close in your notes app: what I heard, what I own, by when, plus the flag-anything line at the end.
  2. Send one real close today for the most recent agreement you made, even if the conversation was days ago. One late close still beats none.
