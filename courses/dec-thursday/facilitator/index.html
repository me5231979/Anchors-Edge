05 · When things go sideways

If it breaks, you have a move

If Screen share or the site fails mid-session: Paste the learner link in chat and narrate from your own browser; every interaction runs on learner devices without the share.
If Breakout rooms are unavailable: Run the breakout prompts as 60-second chat storms: everyone types their answer at once, you read the best three aloud.
If Someone names a real colleague while describing a fuzzy expectation: Protect them warmly and immediately: 'Hold that. Roles, never names.' The moves work on any expectation; the room never needs to know whose.
If A learner says the written close feels passive-aggressive in their team culture: Offer the friendly framing live: 'So I don't drop anything, here is what I have.' A close that reads as self-management lands well everywhere; tone is choosable.

06 · Tough questions, ready answers

Say each answer out loud once before you teach

Won't constant clarity questions annoy busy senior people?

Three targeted questions asked once beat a week of guessing followed by rework they have to review. Busy people benefit most from your clarity. The annoying version is vague questions asked repeatedly, which is exactly what the three-question form prevents.

What if I restate and the other person gets impatient?

Keep it to one sentence and frame it as a service: quick check so I build the right thing. If a ten-second restate still lands badly, that tells you something about the expectation's real clarity, and you just learned it early.

Isn't putting things in writing a trust problem?

The close is written to help both people, and it reads that way when it is short, same-day, and framed as keeping yourself organized. Manager Monday taught the other chair to set expectations visibly; your written close is the matching half of the same standard.

Section 01 · The Ask

Vague requests deserve better questions.

Make it better. Circle back soon. Own this going forward. Requests like these feel clear in the moment and dissolve by Tuesday. The fix is three questions anyone can ask anyone: what does done look like, when is it actually needed, and who makes the call?

The three clarity questions

Before accepting any significant task, get answers to three things: what does done look like, when is it actually needed, and who makes the final call? Asking them is a service to the requester; every one you skip becomes a guess you own.

In the room

Requests like make it better and own this going forward feel clear while everyone is nodding. The clarity lives in three answers: what done looks like, when it is really needed, and who makes the call. Asking serves the requester too: most discover their own picture is fuzzy while answering.

Talk it through: Think of a task that went sideways on you this year. Which of the three answers was missing at the start, and what stopped anyone from asking?

Quick check: asking your manager what done looks like is...

Breakout · 3 min: In pairs: name a current task where you could not describe what done looks like. What would you ask, and of whom? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Name one current task where you cannot describe what done looks like. Write the clarity question you will ask, and to whom.

Go deeper

When the requester does not know yet

Sometimes the honest answer to what does done look like is: I do not know yet. That answer is useful too. It tells you the task is exploratory, so agree on a checkpoint instead of a deliverable: I will bring a rough version Thursday and we will look at it together. And when the asker is senior, bundle the three questions into one sentence that reads as momentum: so I aim this right, what does done look like and by when?

On your own

  1. Write the bundled one-sentence version of the three questions in your own voice, one you could say to the most senior person you work with.
  2. Pick one live task with no picture of done. Decide whether it needs the three questions or a checkpoint agreement, then send the ask today.

  • Assuming it will become clear later

Section 02 · The Restate

The restate finds gaps while they are cheap.

Restating an expectation in your own words is the cheapest test it will ever get. If your version matches theirs, you start with confidence. If it differs, you found the gap while it costs minutes. The judgment call: act, say it back, or get it in writing.

Play · Clear enough?

Why this matters: Three handoffs from a normal week. Call whether you can act, need to say it back, or need it in writing.

Breakout · 3 min: Breakout pairs: name a live handoff in your work that nobody has said back yet. What would your restate be? 90 seconds each way.

On your own: Name a live handoff nobody has said back yet. Write your one-sentence restate of it.

Go deeper

Restating up, down, and sideways

The restate flexes with the relationship. With a peer it can be casual: so I take the data pull and you take the writeup, right? With someone senior, attach it to your next step so it reads as motion: I will start with the survey data, since done means the summary by Friday. And when a handoff has more than three moving parts, restate in writing; a short list is easier to correct than a paragraph of talk.

On your own

  1. Take the live handoff from your solo prompt and write two restates: the casual peer version and the one you would send someone senior.
  2. Use one of them in the real conversation this week. Whatever correction comes back is rework you just saved.
Facilitator scriptFull 10 min · Core 7

Train the restate: judging when an expectation is clear enough to act on and testing it in your own words.

Say

“The restate is the cheapest quality check in working life. You say the expectation back in your own words and end with: am I missing anything? Ninety percent of the time you get a nod and free confidence. The other ten percent you find a gap while it still costs minutes instead of weeks. Let's train the judgment call.”

Do
  1. Run Clear enough? live; everyone plays on their own screen.
  2. Pause after the hallway-agreement round; that one splits the room usefully.
  3. Run the breakout on the handoff nobody has said back.
Ask

“Why can restating a fully clear request backfire?”

Expect:

  • Reads as stalling
  • Signals you were not listening
  • Slows a fast moment

Respond: Right, so the skill includes knowing when to just start. Restate when done, deadline, or decider is missing; act when all three are on the table.

Debrief

Look for done, deadline, decider. Missing any, say it back in your own words. All present, start working.

Watch for: Word-for-word parroting in the examples. Push for own words: parroting proves listening, restating proves understanding.

Section 03 · The Close

Agreements survive in writing.

The last move happens after the conversation: a short written line, same day, saying what you heard and what happens next. Ninety seconds of typing is the difference between an agreement and a memory. Build the close you would actually send.

Practice · Write the close

Why this matters: You just left a planning conversation with real commitments in it. Choose how you restate and how you close the loop.

Choose both steps
Go deeper

Anatomy of a close that settles arguments

A working close reads like this: Thanks for the planning talk. What I heard: full draft to you by the 15th, I own the data section, you own the intro. Flag anything I got off. Notice the last line. The close is a test, and the best result is a correction within the hour, because that reply just saved you the rework. Silence becomes agreement you can point to; a correction is a gap caught while it costs nothing.

On your own

  1. Build your reusable three-line close in your notes app: what I heard, what I own, by when, plus the flag-anything line at the end.
  2. Send one real close today for the most recent agreement you made, even if the conversation was days ago. One late close still beats none.
Facilitator scriptFull 9 min · Core 6

Make the same-day written close a habit: what I heard, what I own, by when.

Say

“Every agreement has a half-life. The conversation was perfect, everyone nodded, and two weeks later there are two honest, different memories of it. The written close stops the decay: three lines, same day. What I heard, what I own, by when. Ninety seconds of typing that replaces hours of rework, so let's build the one you would actually send.”

Do
  1. Run Write the close; give the room two minutes for both choices.
  2. Ask one volunteer to read their two-weeks-later outcome aloud.
  3. Point at the pattern: own words in the room, written words the same day.
Ask

“Why same day, specifically?”

Expect:

  • Memory decays fast
  • Next week you write what you remember
  • It keeps the correction cheap

Respond: Exactly. The close documents the conversation while both memories still match. A week later you are writing fiction in good faith.

Debrief

Three lines, same day: what I heard, what I own, by when. Short enough to always send, specific enough to settle arguments.

Watch for: People drafting long formal recaps. Keep it to three lines; the habit survives only while it stays cheap.
