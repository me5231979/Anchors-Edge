Navigator Workshop · In person, hands on · Coaching as the Core Manager Behavior

Coaching is learned out loud.

Knowing the four beats is not the skill. In this room you coach a real topic in front of an observer and find out which beat stalls when it is your turn to ask.

By the end of this session, you will be able to

  1. Coach a real brought topic through all four beats with an observer tracking each one
  2. Play coachee and observer well: bring a real topic, and track beats instead of solving problems
  3. Name the beat that stalls on you and leave with the question that unsticks it

What we'll cover

Three ideas, in order.

  1. 01

    Set the triads

    Three seats with different jobs, and a real topic each person actually brought.

  2. 02

    Run the rounds

    Live coaching through the four beats, with rotation so every manager sits in every seat.

  3. 03

    Debrief the beats

    Observer notes on the table: where each talk stalled, and the question that would have moved it.

Section 01 · The Triad

Three seats, three jobs.

A triad is a coaching gym. The coach runs the four beats on a real topic, while the observer tracks the beats rather than the problem. The observer often learns most: they hear what the coach cannot.

The observer's rule

Track the beats, not the problem. Note the moment the coach told instead of asked, the beat that got skipped or rushed, and the exact question that moved something. The topic is a prop; the coaching is the work.

In the room

Soon this room becomes a dozen live coaching conversations at once, and the most tempting seat to waste is the observer's. The problem being coached will be interesting; a better idea for solving it will occur to you. Write down beats and questions instead. Your debrief is the coach's mirror.

Talk it through: When you have watched a great coach or a great question at work, what did it actually sound like? And what do you expect to be hardest about staying out of the problem?

Mid-round, the observer spots the coach handing over a solution. The observer's job is...

Grounded in: Coaching conversation research, Center for Creative Leadership

Breakout · 3 min: Form triads across tables, ideally with managers you rarely work with. Coachees: pick the real topic you brought, work-sized, roles only, and say it in one sentence.

Section 02 · The Rounds

Real topics make it real practice.

Rounds run on brought topics because the beats only get tested by something true. The beats are Sir John Whitmore's GROW model: goal first, reality before options, their options before yours, will with a date. Rotate until everyone has coached.

Play · Call the stall

Why this matters: Every coaching talk stalls somewhere. Hear each snippet the observers heard and name the stalled beat.

Grounded in: The GROW model, Sir John Whitmore (Performance Consultants)

Breakout · 3 min: Run the round: coach and coachee work the four beats while the observer writes. Then rotate one seat and start the next round with a fresh topic.

Section 03 · The Debrief

The stall is the finding.

Observers report: where the talk stalled, the moment telling replaced asking, the question that moved something. Then the room compares. Most managers stall on the same beat every time, and naming yours is the most useful thing you take from this room.

Practice · Unstick the beat

Why this matters: You are coaching and the round stalls. Choose your recovery for the two commonest stalls.

Choose both steps

Breakout · 3 min: In your triad: observer reports from notes, beat by beat. Coach names their stall in one sentence and writes the question they will bring to the next real talk.

The core idea

Nobody coaches well in theory. Find your stalled beat and practice that.
