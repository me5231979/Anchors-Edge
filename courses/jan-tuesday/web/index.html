Section 01 · The Green Light

Your week is full of green lights.

AI is at its best on chores: first drafts, tidy summaries, notes turned into clean sentences. The traffic light decides what is safe. Green, public or generic: go. Yellow, internal work: approved VU tools only. Red, private information about people: never.

Play · Call the light

Why this matters: Every use case starts with this call. Read each chore and pick its light.

On your own: Name one chore from your own week and call its light: green, yellow, or red.

Go deeper

Green inside a yellow week

Two refinements make the light easy on real work. Mixed content first: the most sensitive piece in a chore decides the light for the whole task, so one student's name in a page of public text makes the page red. Second, chores can change color. Strip out the internal or personal specifics, run the generic remainder as green, and add the sensitive parts back yourself. Even confidential roles hold a green generic layer.

On your own

  1. List five recurring chores from your calendar and inbox, and mark each one green, yellow, or red.
  2. Take one yellow chore and write its green version: the same task with the specifics stripped out.

Section 02 · The Ask

Ask like you would ask a colleague.

There is a myth that talking to AI takes special training. Plain English works. Tell the tool the job, give it the details that matter, and say what shape you want back. The same sentences you would use to brief a helpful colleague are the ones that work here.

Practice · Build the ask

Why this matters: Your chore: a first draft of a thank-you note to volunteers after a public campus event. Choose what you tell the tool.

Choose both steps

On your own: Write the plain-English ask for the chore you named in section one: the job, the details, the shape.

Go deeper

The second ask

The ask keeps going after the first draft. When it comes back close, say what to change the way you would to a colleague: warmer, shorter, lead with the thanks, drop the last line. Two or three follow-ups usually beat one giant opening ask. One quiet trick: paste in a short sample of something you wrote and ask the tool to match that voice. It mirrors what it is shown, and your own writing is the best voice instruction.

On your own

  1. Run your capstone chore, then send one follow-up that changes a single thing: tone, length, or the opening.
  2. Paste in a paragraph of your own writing, ask for the draft in that voice, and compare the two results.

Section 03 · The Check

You are the editor.

AI drafts with total confidence and zero guarantee. Sometimes it invents a detail, gets a name wrong, or strikes a tone you would never use. The rule that keeps you safe is simple: the draft ships after you have read every line and can stand behind every fact.

The editor's rule

Before an AI draft goes anywhere, check facts (every name, date, and number), fit (is this what you meant to say?), and voice (does it sound like you?). If you cannot verify a detail, cut it or confirm it yourself.

The tool's draft includes a detail you did not provide and cannot verify. What do you do?

On your own: Recall a time a confident-sounding document turned out to be wrong. What would have caught it?

Go deeper

The two-pass check

Make the check a routine and it stays quick. Pass one is facts: touch every name, date, number, and claim, and confirm each against a source you trust; anything you cannot verify gets cut or confirmed by you. Pass two is fit and voice: read the draft aloud once, because your ear catches the sentence you would never say faster than your eye does. On a chore-sized draft the whole routine takes about two minutes.

On your own

  1. Run both passes on your first-win draft and keep a tally of what pass one caught.
  2. Read the final version aloud and rewrite any sentence that does not sound like you.
